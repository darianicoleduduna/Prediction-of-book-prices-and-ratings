天官赐福 [Tiān Guān Cì Fú] #1

Heaven Official's Blessing

Mò Xiāng Tóng Xiù, 墨香铜臭, Suika (Translator)

4.57
52,403 ratings6,454 reviews
Rate this book
Born the crown prince of a prosperous kingdom, Xie Lian was renowned for his beauty, strength, and purity. His years of dedicated study and noble deeds allowed him to ascend to godhood. But those who rise may also fall, and fall he does--cast from the heavens and banished to the world below.

Eight hundred years after his mortal life, Xie Lian has ascended to godhood for the third time, angering most of the gods in the process. To repay his debts, he is sent to the Mortal Realm to hunt down violent ghosts and troublemaking spirits who prey on the living. Along his travels, he meets the fascinating and brilliant San Lang, a young man with whom he feels an instant connection. Yet San Lang is clearly more than he appears... What mysteries lie behind that carefree smile?

This Chinese xianxia fantasy novel series built around the romanticized love between two men (danmei) is the newest work from author Mo Xiang Tong Xiu, and has spawned an ongoing multimedia franchise that's beloved across the world--including the animated series (donghua) that amassed 100+ million views in just its first month. The Seven Seas English-language edition will include covers from 日出的小太陽 (tai3_3), and exclusive, all-new interior illustrations from ZeldaCW.

I think I should start lowering my expectations even for highly rated books on Goodreads, because was it the life-changing reading experience I somewhat assumed it would be based on the rating and reviews where people praised this novel? Well, not for me. Listen, it's not that it wasn't interesting and I was dying of boredom, but at some point I realized that it was simply not for me, as I completely lost interest in whatever was going on here. Unfortunately, this feeling was influenced by a few things, so without further ado, I'll just get to them.

one The first thing that made this story even unreadable for me at times was the translation. It felt like someone had put the entire original novel into Google Translate and expected the result to be perfect—or at least good enough for this book to be published in English. As a person who translated enough Korean and Chinese e-books in Google Translate because I couldn't wait any longer for translations.. this book made me feel like I had gone back in time to a period in my life when I still had the strength to read like that. Constant repetition of certain phrases, such as "I didn't know whether to laugh or cry" or fragments like here

"A son in your prayers
Nan Yang delivers.
Aha ha, aha ha, aha ha ha ha ha ha…”

"This face, with its bulging eyes, was staring straight at him. “Aaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaah!!”
The one who screamed wasn’t Xie Lian or Nan Feng, but that stiff face.

"He said as he coughed, “Cough cough, everyone, this shrine doesn’t bless you with wealth, really, cough, please stop wishing for wealth! The result is unpredictable…"


I just couldn’t take this story seriously because of that.

two Even though I liked Xie Lian's character, he somehow felt flat to me. He was a sweetheart and seeing the story from his point of view was fun and entertaining at times, but I couldn't really connect with him. I mentioned that he was a sweet person, but he also had moments when I started to dislike him

"While he hadn’t interacted much with this girl Xiao-Ying, hadn’t even spoken with her much, Xie Lian still knew that she was a kindhearted girl despite her ugly appearance. Such an ending for her truly made the heart heavy."


Xiao-Ying—the village girl who was treated in the most misogynistic way, constantly called ugly or "too ugly", was dying in the above quote, and Xie Lian, who despite being nice to her, still kept adding that she was "ugly". Was it really necessary? Before him lay a girl on her deathbed, a girl who only wanted to help, and he still kept adding about her "ugly appearance".

“Too ugly!” Fu Yao exclaimed.
Xie Lian choked for a moment, then chided, “Fu Yao, you can’t talk about girls like that.”

I genuinely thought that Xie Lian would be an exception to this girl who was insulted and called ugly all around, but I was wrong. The fact that she was ugly or pretty wasn't even important, so why talk about it?

three The romance part of the book, or rather the slow build up to it, was honestly the only thing I enjoyed here. A romance between a fearsome ghost god who is protective and even flirty, I would say, and Xie Lian, who is a sweet and helpful god? It seemed like something I would definitely enjoy, and I was right.

A Crown Prince, A Ghost King, and the Glory in Ruin🕊️🥀

“One may fall from grace, but not from compassion.”

Some stories ask for your attention.
This one devours your soul.

There’s something sacred about stories that begin with ruin. Heaven Official’s Blessing is not just a story — it’s a soul-deep odyssey.. Through love and despair, fall and rise, sword and flower — it holds your hand through paradise and drops you into the abyss… and you thank it for the fall. This was pain dressed as poetry. This was love dressed as legend.

When the heavens turn their backs and the mortals turn to dust, what remains of a god who has nothing left to offer but kindness? Heaven Official’s Blessing opens not with the thunderous triumph of divinity, but with the quiet, tragic resilience of a once-revered crown prince—Xie Lian, who has ascended to the heavens for the third time, not with reverence but ridicule trailing behind him like tattered robes. The tale begins with laughter at his expense. But what follows is a slow, tender reclaiming of dignity—woven with ghosts, betrayals, vanished brides, and a crimson-eyed stranger who may very well be the most dangerous being in all the realms. - In Heaven Official’s Blessing, pain becomes poetry. Ruin becomes sacred. And love—love becomes a quiet defiance against fate itself.

This isn’t a story you walk away from. It lingers. It loops in your mind like a prayer, a verse, a forgotten name.
Because here, in these pages, every fall is holy.
And every rise is a reminder: some hearts never stay broken. . 🕊️ Xie Lian: The God Who Fell and Still Knelt to Help Others

Crowned as god, yet cursed by fate,
Still he bows low — never filled with hate.

Xie Lian is not your typical hero. His power is muted, his temples in ruins, his name a joke on heavenly lips. Yet, he wears humility like armor and carries the weight of past lives with a smile that does not hide the cracks. Stripped of glory, shackled by divine punishment, he chooses, again and again, to protect, to believe, and to love. I have never read a protagonist quite like him—gentle in despair, dignified in disgrace. He’s fallen from grace so many times, and yet he still smiles. Still helps. Still cares. He’s the kind of character who’s so gentle and tragic that you want to wrap him in a blanket and protect him from the world — but you also know he’d just smile and walk right back into the storm if it meant saving one more soul.

Xie Lian, the once-beloved Crown Prince of Xianle, ascends to the heavens for the third time — now a heavenly joke, a god with no temples and zero worshippers. But this story? It’s not about glory. It’s about grace. About falling hard and rising anyway. When brides begin vanishing, Xie Lian is sent to investigate — and that’s where fate starts twisting. From ghost brides to forgotten kingdoms, from quiet shrines to chaotic heavens, he walks through it all with relentless kindness. Along the way, he meets San Lang — a witty, all-knowing boy who stays by his side through it all. Only… he’s not just anyone. He’s Hua Cheng, the most feared Ghost King, and he’s been waiting for Xie Lian for centuries. And maybe — just maybe — this isn’t a story about gods. It’s about love that remembers, even when the world forgets. . 🌸The Art: A Prayer Made Visible:

Every panel is a breath. Every brushstroke a heartbeat. STARember’s illustrations don’t just draw—they summon. The fabric of Hua Cheng’s robes glides like silk through shadows; Xie Lian’s smile is a soft, tragic curve that feels like forgiveness.

There are moments here that feel cinematic—the red umbrella in the rain, the silver butterflies dancing like promises, the quiet stillness of shared tea. It is gentle and haunting, bright and sorrowful. It is divine. - 🦋 Themes Etched in Silver:
Redemption is not a straight road. Sometimes it’s circular, paved with bones and broken hearts.

Power doesn’t lie in divinity, but in compassion.

Love can be vengeful. And it can be unwavering.

Trust is a silver butterfly landing on your palm—you hold your breath, hoping it stays.

🌬️ And Then There’s the Wind Master… Just when you think the story can’t become more layered, the Wind Master arrives—a reminder of the divine politics brewing above and the game Xie Lian has been unwillingly thrust back into. The gods aren’t omniscient. Some are petty, others compassionate, all walking that fragile line between reverence and irrelevance.

🌹 What Works:
-Art Style: STUNNING. Every panel is a painting, full of rich textures, elegant character designs, and emotionally charged expressions. Xie Lian's grace and Hua Cheng's smirks are brought to life with incredible detail.

-Faithful Adaptation: It stays true to the novel while enhancing key emotional beats visually. Scenes like the iconic umbrella moment? Chef’s kiss.

-Characters: Xie Lian is one of the most lovable tragic figures you’ll ever meet—humble, kind, but carrying centuries of pain with a smile. Hua Cheng? The blueprint for loyal, patient, unshakeable love.

-Humor and Heart: Despite the heavy themes, there’s great banter and moments of lightness. Plus, San Lang (Hua Cheng’s alias) being lowkey flirty while Xie Lian is obliviously polite is endlessly entertaining.

👻 What Might Not Work for Everyone:
Slow Burn: If you're impatient for romance, this story will test you. But oh, the payoff.

Cultural Elements: Some nuances tied to Chinese mythology, heaven rankings, and historical references may need a bit of context if you’re new to xianxia/danmei, but it’s totally worth the effort. - .
✍🏼 MXTX’s Prose: Lyrical, Witty, and Wounding:

To have beauty exist in this world is already in itself something to be grateful for.

I'm a CN/ENG bilingual native and I'm writing this in comparison to the original CN version.

Firstly, on a good note, there are less atrocious mistranslations as compared to MDZS. I've done a cursory sweep and I haven't seen gross MTL level mistakes. Though that's not to say that the translation is entirely accurate either. For example, Chapter 3: "that long, slender, fair hand, it looked like a bright and colorful affinity knot." Uh firstly, in what depiction does Hua Cheng wear a colourful affinity knot? lol The chinese text reads "仿佛一缕明艳的缘结" (as though there was a radiant (明艳) knot). He’s clearly not wearing a colourful ring. Or in Chapter 1 " cultivator passed by and happened to see him sprinkle a handful of dirt to consecrate the grave". The chinese text reads "撒下一抔黄土", when directly translated it means "sprinkle a handful of yellow dust". This phrase just means a grave. There's no act of consecration here.

There are also strange choices of using pinyin/localisations. The locations are have been translated to English. Yet, festivals like Shangyuan has been kept in Pinyin. However, Shangyuan is a widely celebrated festival till this very day and has always been translated to the Lantern Festival worldwide in English text. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lantern...). Did the translation team know this festival exists? There's actually no need to leave it in pinyin.

Secondly, I do feel that most of the vibe has been lost in this translation. The chinese text, particularly the prologue and Chapter 1, is written in a humorous manner. It has a "Let me tell you the story of this weird ass crown prince. He did etc etc etc, can you believe it? And when his teachers told him that he needs to be punished, he said ‘nope lol'. And wtf he's coming up here again?!" I'm really doubtful if anyone would catch all this if they read this translation. Also, the prose in this translation still feels very strange (though slightly smoother than their MDZS translation), but I'd leave that to other reviews to elaborate on it.

There are also parts that seemed to be omitted from the original text. For example, the team seemed to have missed a deliberate mirroring by MXTX. In Chapter 1 there are two lines that read "And thus, the crown prince replied with his now-famous line: “Body in the abyss, heart in paradise.” and "The Emperor answered, “This child’s future is infinite.”. The original text reads "太子就说了著名的八个字" 'The Prince says the famous 8 characters,' and '帝君也答了八个字' The Emperor replied with 8 characters'." This is vital to the story. Also, to be accurate, 无间 should be translated as Avici (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Av%C4%ABci) instead of abyss and 桃源 as Peach Blossom Spring/Taoyuan (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pea....) Taoyuan also carries the idea of a temporary utopia whereby people within are oblivious to the outside world. It implies that this state doesn’t last long)

TLDR, I still think this is a poor representation of the original chinese text. For the record, the original TGCF is better written than MDZS language wise. (MXTX has polished up her writing quite a bit). Maybe I'll give it 2.5 for less MTL errors. Unfortunately, the one and only fan translator did the official translation, so I guess non cn natives in this fandom will never know what the original book was supposed to sound like.

Also read volume 2; this is one continuous story in several volumes, not yet complete in English translation, not a story and sequels. Its original publication history as a serialized web novel affects its structure in interesting ways, as commerce always does art. My own serialized novels back in the days of paper magazines had to be completely finished before the editor would/could purchase them, and had length and cut-point constraints; they had to be under 100,000 words, and divide into monthly chunks sensibly.

Web novels, I understand (I haven't seen one on the hoof yet) are published like manga, in ongoing chapters composed on the fly, with understandable challenges to later continuity issues. In manga, this was always with the threat of being abruptly cut off mid-story if the popularity fell, as the paper magazines had tight "shelf space" for which other works were always competing. I don't know how the far more fungible length consideration of pixels affects this, but Asian web novels appear to descend from this magazine culture rather than from book publishing.

Again, I came to this work backwards; the animation first, then the novel/s. I found them somewhat complementary. The 13-episode animation (found on Netflix) gave visuals, but no explanations. The novel, written in a blunt, breezy omniscient, supplies welcome loads of explanations, but not much description, immersion, or interiority. As in the prior work I've read in translation from this author, Grandmaster of Demonic Cultivation, https://www.goodreads.com/review/show... the end notes and glossary in the back are worth the price of admission for someone new to what it teaches one is the xianxia genre, a well-developed Chinese fantasy tradition devoted to tales of Daoist magicians, gods, demonology, etc.

"Danmei" is its further subcategory, what Japanese media dubs "boy's love", romantic but not explicit m/m tales. Somehow, when all those early highbrow preceptors were urging Americans to read more foreign fiction in translation to improve our cultural parochialism, I don't think danmei was what they had in mind... Though I have to admit it's working.

The romance in this one is the apparently universal classic of Beauty & the Beast (the other one is Cinderella), cranked up to 11. In this case, between the good-girlboy minor god Xie Lan, with 800 years of angsty backstory behind him, and bad-boy demon Hua Cheng, ditto. Not to be mapped directly onto Western conceptions of demons as fallen angels, though the ascending of humans to gods reminds me a bit of what was done with deceased Roman emperors. Heaven in Daoist mythology is imagined as an imperial bureaucracy, pretty exactly parallel to how heaven is imagined as a kingdom in Western myths; as below, so above. Xie Lan's struggles with his own fantastical bureaucracy are quite human. (And familiar -- the gods' magical Zoom meetings are a stitch.)

Together, the heroes fight magical crime, paced in manga-style (manhua-style, in the proper Chinese vocabulary) on-flowing story arcs. With zombies. This author seems rather big on zombies, tho' that's likely a standard genre trope. I can see the appeal -- lots of bad guys available on demand for the good guys to dramatically shred, without the moral complexity of massacring humans, although in these tales the body count among live, well, briefly live, humans is also high. And gruesome. Definitely horror elements. (But there's an afterlife, so it's... kinda ok?) The story so far is getting around to the romance rather gradually.

The first volume was a slog for me, as the anime (donghua, properly) version already in my head competed with the prose on the page. The second volume, where I did not know what was going to happen, worked much better. The translation style continues to make my inner copy editor twitch. So many word echoes. So much tin ear -- at one point a character exclaims "Jeeze!", which made me wonder if the Jesuits had been there by that time... (And was that in the original, and if it wasn't, what was that word-choice representing?) So many startling punctuation shortcuts imported from manga custom, such as the "..." for a nonplussed pause, about which I have mixed feelings. (Amusingly, the recently read https://www.goodreads.com/review/show... touches on this evolutionary process.)

Whatever. I will certainly be reading the third volume to find out what happens next, so I will call this a successful read.

This is my first danmei, but not my first Boy's Love read. I prefer Girl's Love, but I still seek some Boy's Love every now and then. Fortunately, it wasn't a complete let down but not the 5/5 read I hoped for.

Pacing

The story felt a tad too slow at the start. At around chapter 3 it picked up again and stayed medium-paced till the end.

Writing Style

This is the part that lacked the most and made the story feel more unengaging than it could have been.

I don't understand Chinese, so I don't know for certain if this is only a translation issue or the actual writing style of the author. I will pretend it's a translation issue 'cause it felt at times like it failed to catch the tone the author originally tried to convey to the reader.

Main themes
Devotion, loyalty, compassion, kindness and resilience.

Other themes
Corruption, reputation and dignity.

Characters

The fallen god Xie Lian is kind and has lots of compassion to share. To dare speak of the people of the world, whether to save or to destroy them, is admirable. The former is harder than the latter, so it’s even more respectable.

Perdón, pero acabo de leer una reseña que dice que en este libro no pasa mucho. Procedo a desmentirlo.

Podríamos decir que La bendición del oficial del cielo (Tian Guan Ci Fu) es una novela de fantasía épica con raíces en la espiritualidad china, y no estaría mal. Es una historia, hasta cierto punto, de héroes, dioses y guerreros. Pero yo no diría que ese es el centro de la trama. En todo caso, TGCF trata de la humanidad de un dios caído —y vuelto a ascender.

Quedé tan enamorada del mundo de cultivadores, espíritus, dioses y demonios que quiero hacer una investigación exhaustiva sobre el taoísmo, el budismo y el folklore chino. Porque en esta casa no andamos con medias tintas. Rezo para tener una materia sobre la literatura china en general y xianxia en particular.

Primero, un poco de contexto:

El universo de TGCF se basa en la cosmología del taoísmo, donde los humanos pueden cultivar su espíritu (estos son llamados cultivadores) para alcanzar la inmortalidad y ascender al Cielo como deidades. El reino celestial tiene un sistema burocrático sumamente organizado y jerarquizado, lleno de rangos, normas y disputas de poder. Tienen hasta una sala para comunicarse telepáticamente, amusing.

Y en este mundo también conviven los personajes típicos del folklore chino: fantasmas, demonios y dioses locales con sus templos y ofrendas. Estoy encantada.

Algo interesante es que los dioses, aunque se supone que llegaron al Cielo por su virtud y cultivación espiritual, no representan esa pureza para nada. Son (casi) todos una banda de corruptos y vanidosos.

💕 Xie Lian💕 es la excepción. Ocho siglos antes, era el amado príncipe heredero del reino de Xianle: valiente, justo, compasivo y bondadoso. Alcanzó el ascenso celestial a los diecisiete años. Y también era el tipo de persona que, cuando alguien le dijo que no quería vivir más, respondió: “Si no tienes nada por lo que vivir, hazlo por mí. Si no sabes cuál es el sentido de tu vida, entonces, por favor, tómame a mí como tal. Haz que yo sea el pilar que te sostiene.” No sé, yo lo amo en todas sus versiones.

Sin embargo, su deseo de salvar a todos lo llevó a intervenir en guerras y asuntos mortales, desafiando el equilibrio del Cielo. Esto tiene sentido porque, en el taoísmo, el Dao es el orden natural del universo, que representa la armonía y el equilibrio de todas las cosas. La idea principal es vivir en sintonía con ese orden a través del wu-wei (la no acción), dejando que las cosas fluyan naturalmente en lugar de forzarlas… y nuestro querido Xie Lian se negó a no intervenir en la guerra. Por eso su reino cayó, sus seguidores lo abandonaron y fue expulsado del reino celestial, reducido a la pobreza y la soledad.

Y es genial, porque en ese sentido Xie Lian encarna tanto el ideal budista de la compasión infinita como el desequilibrio al que se opone el taoísmo. Su caída no es un castigo divino per se, sino el resultado de su exceso de virtud. Y OJO, PARECE CHAMUYO, PERO ESTUVE LEYENDO Y NO ES INVENTO MÍO.

Cientos de años después, tras un segundo ascenso y otra caída, Xie Lian vuelve al reino celestial. Nada más llegar, logra ganarse enemigos. Nadie lo respeta, nadie cree en él Es “el hazmerreír de los tres reinos”, desprestigiado por haber sido desterrado dos veces —algo inaudito— y porque, además, se ganó la vida como recolector de chatarra en el mundo terrenal.

Ahora, Xie Lian no tiene el mismo orgullo desbordado que lo llevó a desafiar el orden por salvar a su pueblo, pero todavía quiere hacer las cosas bien. Quiere ayudar a la gente. Conserva su humildad y sentido del humor. Soporta las humillaciones, cuando cualquier otro los habría molido a palos por forros. Xie Lian es EL ejemplo de armonía💫. Y ojo a ese aspecto suyo, porque también si entendemos los ascensos y descensos como etapas de caer y volver a levantarse, encajan perfectamente con la idea de la cultivación espiritual. La segunda vez que Xie Lian ascendió, estaba enojadísimo —al punto de atacar a los otros dioses— y lo volvieron a expulsar, pero cuando asciende por tercera vez, ya no parece enojado, ni siquiera cuando tendría motivos.

((Y Xie Lian llega a ese estado rechazando su pasado, pero… ¿qué va a pasar cuando se de cuenta de que, en realidad, no hizo nada verdaderamente malo? Que solo quiso actuar por el bien de su corazón, mientras los demás dioses son más corruptos y egoístas de lo que aparentan… just saying)).

Pero bueno. Más allá de todo, Xie Lian sabe que tiene que arreglar las cosas. Para eso, vuelve a bajar al reino terrenal —esta vez como inmortal— y lo hace con toda la valentía del mundo. Ahí, nuestro honorable príncipe heredero investiga unas desapariciones atribuidas al “novio fantasma” del Monte Yushi. No vamos a ser serios con eso, es solo el primer libro de una saga de ocho. Y tengo muchas ganas de leer cada uno.

No amé la traducción, pero sí quedé encantada con las descripciones, los personajes y esas escenas de terror que no me esperaba para nada. A veces es épico, otras espiritual y a veces hermoso, tierno y muy humano.

I had a hard time at the beginning/middle because of the lack of inner thoughts. It’s difficult to connect with a main character that’s nice with everyone and shows nothing of his true feelings. Being a translation from Chinese, it also felt like we were losing a big part of the story, but that's a price I'm willing to pay to be able to witness it. However, once San Lang appeared I didn’t mind neither of them that much because I was too busy fangirling. I adored with all my heart that, from the first second, Lang’s single purpose in life seemed to be taking care of Xie Lian, defending him against any kind of emotional or physical attack. I have a feeling there is a lot of history I’m not grasping because as it was, it seemed like Lang was in love with Xie Lian before they even interacted. Not that I could blame him; if someone deserves to be loved in an instant is Xie Lian with his beautiful, beautiful heart.


Overall, I liked this introduction to the series. I’m more than ready to suspend all my common sense, suffer a heart attack due to the cheesiness and witness every single moment between San Lang and Xie Lian. If you are looking for a slow burn for hopeless romantics like me, I truly recommend getting on board!

———my thoughts———

Born the crown prince of a prosperous kingdom, Xie Lian’s years of noble deeds allowed him to ascend to godhood.
But whose who rise can also fall, and so he does—cast from the heavens and banished to the world below.
Ascending to godhood for the third time, he is sent to repay his debts in the Mortal Realm, where he meets a fascinating young man named San Lang.
But San Lang has secrets, and who knows what hides under his carefree smile?

This is one of those books that need to be read in one sitting, or in large chunks.
It took me so long to get through the first 150 pages because I kept getting distracted, but when I just sat down and read? I enjoyed it far more.
The writing—the translation—is majorly what I found difficult. I’ve never really read very many translations before, just a couple here and there.
But this is my first in a while.
The writing jumped between being extremely formal to informal!
We went from “a terrible occurrence” to “yeah, I guess” and it was quite jarring at the beginning.
It also felt like there was so much world building that I was getting lost...maybe I’m just not smart enough, but I struggled to keep track of everything at the beginning.
And then I discovered the glossary.
Good god, that thing saved my life.
I understand (mostly) everything now, and I think I enjoyed it more because of it.
Plot-wise, there was a lot going on...but also nothing happened?
(Although I did feel very smart seeing through a couple plot points)
But one thing that really bothered me was how everyone treated Xiao-Ying. Everyone called her ugly, and nobody but Xie Lian cared when she died.
Like what the fuck? There are barely any female characters in this anyway, but treating one of them like that? No.
But I would say that the sort-of-budding romance is pretty cute!
It made me smile a couple times, and the cover is drop dead GORGEOUS.
I’ll be coming back for the other books...but not immediately.
