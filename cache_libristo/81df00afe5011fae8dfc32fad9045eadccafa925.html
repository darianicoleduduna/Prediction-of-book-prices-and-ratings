Sigh.
I’m getting tired of second installments that manage to dismantle everything that made me excited about the first one.

But I’m going to write a bad review this time, even if I don’t enjoy doing that, because this book doesn’t deserve its 4.59 rating.
FOUR POINT FIFTY-NINE!

Let me start by saying that Sarah A. Parker created a stunning world. It’s imaginative. The central premise is fantastic. The mythology of fallen dragon moons, the mystery surrounding Raeve’s identity, the history woven through Elluin’s journals, the political intrigue, the dragons themselves—loved it all. Book #1 had flaws, but it had wonder. It had momentum. It made me eager to turn the page. Book #2 made me acutely aware of how many pages were left to turn.

The biggest problem is that there is no room to breathe. Every scene, every conversation, every internal monologue is written at maximum emotional intensity and maximum sentence length (more on that later). Everyone is shattered, broken, fractured, devastated, yearning, unraveling, aching, burning, or some combination thereof. But—dammit—when every moment is treated as monumental, nothing feels monumental anymore. The story is in desperate need of quieter scenes and emotional contrast. Every chapter opens with a revelation, a crisis, a confrontation, or some other attempt to shock the reader awake. After a while, my reaction ceased to be “Oh no!” and became “Oh for fuck's sake.” You know that feeling of listening to a fire alarm for 800 pages? Yup, that’s the one.

The prose is (in my opinion) the main culprit. It was already overindulgent in Book #1, and it already bothered me then, but here it becomes downright EXHAUSTING. “Brevity is the soul of wit,” Shakespeare wrote. Judging by this sequel, verbosity is the soul of cardio. The plot itself isn’t nearly as complicated as the prose makes it feel (at least not initially) and certainly doesn’t justify the enormous page count. But every feeling is filtered through layers and layers of metaphors and similes and hyperboles and poetic imagery and whatever other literary devices Parker had in her arsenal. Nothing is allowed to simply BE. A character can't be sad—they have to be a storm trapped inside a cage of fractured moonlight while grief claws molten talons through the shattered architecture of their soul. Whatever. After hundreds of pages like this, all I could do was notice the writing instead of the story again and glare at it because I didn’t even remember what the beginning of the paragraph was about.

Then there’s the worldbuilding vocabulary. I’ve lost count of the number of times this series introduced words with little contextual grounding. I kept stopping to wonder whether I was looking at an obscure English word, a creature, a profession, a title, or a typo. Instead of immersing me in the world, it yanked me out of it.
And perhaps most frustrating of all: the multiple POVs where everyone sounds exactly the same. Despite their vastly different backgrounds and personalities, Kaan, Raeve, Veya, Kyzari all process the world through the same highly poetic, metaphor-heavy voice and seem perpetually one “fucking” or “Creators-dammit” away from the same furious tantrum. If chapter headings disappeared, I genuinely wouldn’t know who was “fucking” talking.

Other things that irritated me big time but this review is already too long:

◉ Using the gods’ tongue all the freaking time just to translate it afterward. Then, somewhere around the halfway mark, Parker decided we’ve all achieved conversational fluency and stopped giving a fuck about translating.

◉ To deepen the mythology (and lengthen the series, I figure), Book #2 is attaching additional compartments to an already freight train.

◉ What was the point of Nee’s death? To show that the Scavenger King is a big, bad, mean villain?

The more I wonder whether my own softness is conditioned or innate. And Raeve is an FMC whose fire is provoked, earned, and conditioned. Even though she lives in a fantasy world spawned from somewhere inside Sarah’s incredible mind, she faces some of the same challenges women have across the ages: limited choices, gender inequality, the arrogant assumption that males have a say in her reproductive rights.

Initially I thought Raeve’s emotional isolation made her unapproachable. But after rereading WtMH I saw her differently. Raeve’s story is for anyone who has bottled away their emotions and trauma, who has kept others at a distance (even loved ones) in order to avoid being a burden or experiencing more hurt. She locks everything down because she just feels so deeply.

Maybe it’s the way she is willing take pain or punishment in any form in her effort to protect those she loves. Or her deep sorrow and rage over the injustices wrought against the innocent and vulnerable.

*Disclaimer: I’m not saying you have to be a mom to enjoy this or that all moms will. I think reading is such a personal experience and I’m only explaining my own viewpoint.

Overall this is a stunning sequel even with all heart wrenching pain. And if there’s another two years between books, I’ll be just as excited for the next.

Yes Sarah, I did. I felt EVERY. single. beat.
