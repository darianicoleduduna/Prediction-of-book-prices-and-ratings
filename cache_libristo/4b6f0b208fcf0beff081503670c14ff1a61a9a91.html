This is such an impactful read that brings up so many questions about exploitation (in many different ways), current trends in the publishing industry, what we seek as humans, and so much more.

Not only are we following a character who is literally stealing and exploiting someone else's story for their own gain, but we on the other side - are following a character tugging on others' emotions also for their own gain. Because of this, we get this situation where everyone is actually using each other in different ways. I think having 2 main characters who are both flawed and who were both exploiting each other for their own gain made this SUCH an interesting read, and helped deviate from some other books in this sub-genre. If this was just your usual story about one unlikeable character using a perfect angel character, I feel like it wouldn't have felt as nuanced - so that was a great choice!

It also pointed out so many trends in the publishing industry like... why are women SO pulled towards the lives and stories of gay men right now? On the other side, why do we all seek out PAIN in books? Why is a book only good if it makes us cry? (guilty haha)

Josh Silver's writing is quite erratic in this book, which I think felt very genuine to the story and characters and really felt like we were in their heads. The only reason I think this wasn't a 5 star for me is it did feel a little long around the middle point and maybe felt a just a little repetitive for a tiny bit.

This book covers heavy topics, and does not shy away from them. Tread carefully if themes of substance abuse, addiction, suicide are triggers for you. Overall, such a great book - definitely opens up so many conversations, and I do love how things thematically and plot-wise are left a bit open ended. It's a book that doesn't spoon feed you, and encourages you to think for yourself.

Worth the hype - definitely recommend!

Who decides what we can be?

Who decides what we can write about?

This is a novel written by a gay man about a woman (Mallory) who pretends to befriend a young gay man (Leo), addicted to drugs, in order to write his story that might revitalize her dying career. But "pretending" is a word that tells only half the story. Mallory and Leo are pretending so much that sometimes they lie not only to others, but to themselves. They aren't happy. People see Mallory as a modern Clarissa Dalloway, but she feels more like Virginia Woolf trapped in a Sleeping with the Enemy marriage, always forced to smile and do (and write) what others want. Leo is homeless, a thief, a sex worker, a poet, a liar so accustomed to selling his dramatic life story to benefactors that he can't even realize he's in an A Little Life situation.

The beauty of this disturbingly crazy, fun, hopeful novel is that it understands a great story between two complex characters like these can't be stopped by the pretending. We do that in real life too, and we still find a way to each other when we are soulmates. And these two are soulmates. They need each other to survive, to escape, and to become who they want to be.

One of the most powerful scenes in the book is Leo's reaction when he finds out the horrible ending the publisher wants for the character inspired by him. It reminds us how important it is to see ourselves succeed, even if only in books or films. It gives hope, and we need that, but this book is more than a critique of the publishing world.

This book is an act of rebellion. It understands the power of representation, but it wants to remind us that we can be who we want to be, no matter what others think we should be. It is offensively funny, disturbingly funny, too real to be able to laugh, and so tense in the second half that it gave me anxiety. The finale was so strange, because we get an amazing ending and then something feels off, but maybe not. This is book of the year material for me

If that made very little sense, welcome to my experience reading Fruit Fly.

Going into it, I felt a little out of my depth. I've never really read anything quite like it before, and for a good chunk of the book, I honestly had no idea what to make of it. Being inside Leo's head is a lot. He's chaotic, impulsive, self-destructive and sometimes completely bloody ridiculous. One minute I wanted to tell him to please just stop talking, and the next I wanted to reach into the book and save him from himself. Underneath all the humour and erratic behaviour is someone carrying around a hell of a lot of pain, and the further I got, the harder it became not to see everything else as something he was using to bury it.

Then there's Mallory. I can't say I particularly liked her, but I did find her interesting, especially in the ways she tried to justify her questionable behaviour. She's desperate for something to write about, and Leo and the world around him give her exactly that. The problem is how far she's willing to worm her way into spaces and people's lives that aren't hers in order to get it. There was something about watching her recognise that she was crossing a line and then find another reason to keep going anyway that gave me the ick. By that point, I couldn't help thinking that if you're willing to stir the pot, don't complain when it boils over.

And I think that's where the biggest question in Fruit Fly lies: who gets to tell whose stories?

My first instinct was that some stories simply aren't yours to tell, but the more I thought about it, the less clear-cut that answer became. Writers step outside their own experiences all the time. People write about different cultures, sexualities, marginalised communities, vulnerable people and lives completely unlike their own. So where exactly is the line? Is it the story itself that's the problem, or how you went about telling it? When does curiosity or inspiration turn into exploitation, and at what point does observing someone become using them?

I still don't have a neat answer, and maybe that's the point. What I do know is that the way Mallory went about it made me uncomfortable, particularly when friendship, curiosity and self-interest became so tangled together. There were moments when I wondered whether she genuinely cared about Leo, saw him as material, or somehow managed to do both at the same time.

There are quite a few triggers in this one, so I'd definitely recommend checking the content warnings before going in.

Fruit Fly was strange, messy, funny, bleak and occasionally exhausting. I'm not sure I understood everything Josh Silver was throwing at me, and for much of it I wasn't even sure how I felt about what I was reading. But I finished it thinking about ownership, exploitation and the stories we take from other people's lives, and I'm still trying to answer some of the questions it left behind.

Apparently I even came out the other side writing poetry.

I definitely need a detox after that.

Content/Trigger Warnings: **may contain ‘spoilers’**, anxiety, anxiety attacks, blackouts, addiction, assault, abuse, abusive parents, domestic abuse, homophobia, hallucinations, swearing, substance addiction, suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, sexual assault, stalking, on page gang-rape, mental illnesses, mentions of former psychiatric admission (These were all I noted, but there is no guarantee that I haven't missed anything. This is also a very heavy book, which is why I am not tagging these as ‘spoilers’, so please take care while reading. Mention and discussion of these in the review.)

I was intrigued by Fruit Fly from the moment I read the description, and when I saw it was available on NetGalley Read Now I downloaded and read it instantly. The first 30 pages of the book were absolutely fantastic, and had me convinced that this would not only be a 5 star read, but also be a new all time favourite. Unfortunately, as the book progressed, it turned out to be a disappointment for me.

THE PLOT

Former famous bestselling author Mallory has been stuck in a “writer's block” for years. Desperate to resurrect her career, she decides to follow Reddit's simple advice “Go gay. Gay sells.” But as Mallory meets Leo, a gay man struggling with addiction and mental illnesses, the question arises, how far is she willing to go for the perfect story?

MY THOUGHTS

Fruit Fly had an interesting concept. The underlying messaging, of who gets to write stories of marginalised people, and “who gets to profit off them” is definitely a conversation worth having. The first 30 pages in particular, are very well written. However for me, the book goes downhill from there. Many readers are calling this the “gay Yellowface”, and while I do see the resemblance, I don't feel this goes hard enough on any of its messaging. I loved Yellowface to the point of giving it 5 stars, and yet I found that too to be lacking in certain aspects of its social commentary, and Fruit Fly is even more so. (Also this is dual POV, unlike that one, just a heads up!)

I get that this book is meant to be a satire with mystery/thriller vibes. And it worked, at first. Mallory being the “clueless straight women” venturing into the “gay world” made for an amusing start, in contrast to the darker tone the book set up with Leo's POV. But there's only so much of that one can take, before it eventually gets boring.

It did not help that to me, the characters were almost caricatures, in the sense that they read “two-dimentional”, there was a lack of emotional depth. Without spoiling too much, both these characters go through their own journeys of struggle, except neither feels “authentic” enough. There is just this major lack of connection or even feeling towards either of them. I saw another review (https://www.goodreads.com/review/show...) saying that Leo reads like the character Liam that Mallory wrote, and not like his own person, and I completely agree. Now, before you argue that was the point of the book, or the intention with this choice, if that was the case, it did not do a good enough job, and to be very blunt, I do not think it was smartly written enough for that kind of a full circle impact. I do think certain aspects like Leo's addiction was written well enough, and the abuse Mallory faces was actually almost terrifying to read. But ultimately, I was left wanting more. Also this was filled with so many pop culture references that just did not work for me.

My other complaint is the length. This book was too long for the story it was trying to tell. Now, don't get me wrong, I am not saying that topics or themes like this don't warrant a long book, just that this one in particular would have been a way better novella. I feel the longer the pages, the more this just dragged, with one bad event after the other happening to the characters. And I do not mind dark stories, but at a certain point, as harsh as it's going to sound, I just felt that there was almost no reason for this trauma, because we don't see enough of the healing, and most of the “development” of these fictional characters happens off page. And look, again, I am not saying that people shouldn't have trauma represented in fiction or that darker stories shouldn't exist as they are. All I am saying is that all the events of this book could have had the same, and even better impact if this was like 160 pages. If anything it would have made for a very sharp read. Unfortunately, I think many readers are going to get bored of this story post 20% and not complete it.

Another small gripe I have is I do not quite get this Book Title. Maybe there is something that I missed, but why is Fruit Fly called Fruit Fly? (Edit 5/6/26: The lovely Ash told me that 'Fruit Fly' is a word used for straight women fetishisising gay men. That makes the title make a lot of sense, its actually quite clever! Though i wish the book incorporates a mention of this in the Author's Note or something. Ofc not the responsibility of the author to educate, but this would be missed by many and adds a lot to the book! Also makes the Yellowface comparisons make more sense. I am changing my rating from 2 star to 2.5 star bec of this additional context.)

However, it was not an awful book, and I have read way worse. The writing was pretty good. I also somewhat liked the ending, but I think many people will not.

Overall, Fruit Fly did not quite work for me, but I do appreciate this book for making me have enough thoughts to write such a long review (it's been a while!) AND making a book playlist. I do think a niche audience is going to love and resonate with this story, as I am seeing with the reviews, so don't let me discourage you from reading it. And of course this is just my opinion, and if you like the book good for you, but please don't start being rude in my comments, you will be blocked. I am definitely going to read more from this author, and even would read a sequel to this one, as long as it's shorter in length.

5/5 - “It fucking hurts. All of it. But at least I feel it.”

I am in awe, speechless. This book was UNREAL. It’s been some time since I’ve been truly hooked by a writing style alone, and Josh Silver’s scratched the right itch for me. His ability to envelop me into the chaos of Mallory’s mind was riveting, and his intentional deep dive into the true nature of the gay experience was fresh, authentic and perspective-shifting.

There are a lot of gay/queer books in the current literary world that really tailor to straight and heteronormative culture, but this book stands out amongst those. Silver’s unfiltered approach to queerness, the pursuit of identity and fulfillment in companionship was groundbreaking for me here. The juxtaposition between straight and queer domestic partnerships was done so well, really diving into the nuances that is experiencing love and life as a queer individual.

This does not shy away from the brutality that can come with finding yourself, bringing forth a conversation about the gay experience that not many are privy to, or completely understand. I'm always inherently a bit skeptical when a book takes the publishing world by storm, and rarely does it convince me to pick up a book I otherwise wouldn't have read. Fruit Fly was the exception — curiosity got the best of me, and admittedly, I thought I'd land somewhere in the middle with my rating of it. Josh Silver proved me wrong. This was superb. One of my few five-star reads of the year, and a book that certainly lives up to the hype.

The novel interrogates our collective tendency to center queer stories around suffering and trauma, while also questioning who we allow the platform and authority to tell these stories. It feels especially meta coming from Silver, who worked as a mental health nurse before the publication of the book, and has also remarked on his parallel experiences to Leo. It gives the reader enough trust that this is exactly who should be telling this story.

It's hard to talk about why this novel is so successful without veering into spoiler territory, but I'll say that the final ~20% of this book was entirely unexpected and stuck the landing. I got a little nervous halfway through, wondering if this would turn into some banal, domestic thriller: but I'm happy to admit I was proven wrong. It's especially ironic thinking of the ending in light of the runaway success of the book (which I do think has been largely organic — I don't get the sense even the publisher expected this to do as well as it has, given the limited print run and minimal marketing budget).

Part of me is also interested to see how the legacy of this book evolves. Anecdotally, I've seen a lot of heterosexual readers being the most vocal critics of this (to be clear, this is not me critiquing them), and part of me wonders if the "meta-ness" of it is largely tied to the identity of the reader and how they relate to the story? Unsure. It's hard to deny I'm biased in my tendency to like this as a gay man, yet I do think this is more clever than some readers are giving it credit for.
