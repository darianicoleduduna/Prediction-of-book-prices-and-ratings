Enter the library of an enigmatic collector, where occult books are guarded and researched at a fatal cost, with this uncanny eldritch horror novel.

Fear comes before the fall.

In the winding, perilous corridors of Jurgen Leitner’s private library, you never know what you’ll find. A starving cookbook. An ancient scroll that casts cool, predatory breezes. A cloth-bound hardback that compels you to DIG, DIG, DIG, DIG.

Free, these books are more than simply unnerving—they’re deadly. But the library keeps their dangers in check and their would-be readers safe. . . .

Or so Leitner claims.

For two of his employees, the risks are worth it. Hugh Franklin, research assistant, has finally found a place he belongs, where his skill in translation is valued, even if his employer is a bit odd. Sebastian Everett, acquisition specialist, can indulge his arcane ambitions, even if it means pretending to care about Leitner’s mission.

Though their tenures at the library were years apart, Hugh and Sebastian’s stories unfold in parallel, and their footsteps echo down the same eerie aisles, caught in a web spun long before either ever heard the name “Jurgen Leitner.”

Will they find a way out . . . or will the library consume them before it’s too late?

Cosmic horror with occult mystery

From the Library of Jurgen Leitner by Premee Mohamed belongs to the Magnusverse, a multifaceted dark fantasy entertainment podcast by Jonathan Sims. Jurgen Leitner is a wealthy collector of magical books. The books are dangerous and dubious, suggesting a supernatural horror larger and more uncontrollable than anything we know from the natural world. The books can take a psychological and physical hold on someone and cause serious damage. Books of all shapes and sizes take on malevolent properties either by spell, incantation, or some other cosmic magic. Jurgen Leitner is a mysterious yet magnetic fellow. He hires assistants to work in his library to contain the books (keep humanity safe from them)and perform experiments on them-various tests to determine what the books can do and how they need to be shelved for optimal flow. He hires researchers, translators, academics, and otherwise gullible individuals who ultimately risk their lives in his library! He goes through a lot of employees and it's not from early termination or a high turnover rate. These books are seriously dangerous and cause so many supernatural terrors from radiation poisoning, meet grinding, compulsive digging, even changing ones very state of consciousness.

And I haven't even gotten to the good part! Deliciously unsettling...

Where this book and the writing really shine is the portrayal of the two protagonists in alternating timelines between 1992-1994. (Those were great years, btw). First we have Hugh Franklin, a translator eager to exert his expertise in the world and finally secure a steady job. Anxious and accommodating, he really doesn't believe in the books at first. But colleagues die! Right in front of their eyes. And this Leitner guy only asks "was the book damaged?" I am going to have to start listening to the podcast because apparently many of the magical qualities of the books here are related to stories that show up in the podcast; hence this complex web of stories of the Magnusverse. I really like Hugh's character a lot. His descent into madness as orchestrated by the powers of the books was devastating. The tie in of the story lines was intriguing.

Then we have Sebastian Everett in a later timeline. Sebastian has aspirations and ambitions. Ultimately this story is about the risk of intellectual ambitions. He has goals, connections, and ideas. He is interested in Leitner in a different way and will find a way to usurp the library through his connections and manipulation of malevolent aspects of the books. Sebastian himself is responsible for murdering his colleagues! His usurpation of the library is not as neat and clean as he would have liked! He is tangled in a web of cosmic terror.

I found the mysterious and forbidden knowledge in the books of this universe to be extremely seductive, and their magical effects vivid and dreadful! I was so entertained I immediately started again for a second time. I enjoy how the alternating perspectives of Leitner create a complex picture of the menace behind the madness.

5/5 re-read

Some books are dangerous to read. Others are dangerous to even open. And then there are the books that will make you wish you’d never laid eyes on them at all. My thanks to Random House Worlds for the arc.

From the Library of Jürgen Leitner by Premee Mohamed and Jonathan Sims is a deliciously unsettling descent into a gothic library where books can kill, madness comes in a myriad of forms and even the most innocuous looking volume may be dangerous in ways that are truly horrifying.

From gruesome deaths and psychological torment to stranger, more inexplicable effects, the sheer variety of horrors lurking within these pages is a delight for anyone with a taste for the sinister. Just as fascinating are the methods devised to contain, combat and survive these dangerous tomes, each carrying its own peculiar menace.

The library itself is a marvel of atmosphere, where dark academia meets cosmic horror and the pursuit of forbidden knowledge brushes against the incomprehensible. Ominous dread seeps through the shelves but amid the supernatural dangers are the mundane banalities of life as a lowly intern. There is something darkly absurd about navigating the everyday indignities of a job when the books around you could drive you mad or kill you in any number of inventive ways.

Beneath the creeping horror lies a story of ambition and avarice, of secrets guarded and betrayals waiting to surface. Two interwoven timelines follow Hugh and Sebastian, whose very different attitudes towards the Library gradually come to light. As their motivations and goals emerge, the mystery takes shape and their paths converge. Each revelation draws the threads tighter, building towards a conclusion that is supremely satisfying.

Although connected to The Magnus Archives podcast this is a thoroughly compelling horror novel in its own right and requires no prior familiarity with the podcast to appreciate its sinister delights.

For those who love their libraries gothic, their books deadly and their horror steeped in creeping dread, this is a macabre invitation to turn the page. Just be careful what you read.

The atmosphere was the sort of looming and heavy darkness that wraps around the reader and drags them into the story. The vibes were labyrinthian, and paired with the gothic, isolated setting - even something as mundane as cataloging books became an exercise in psychological survival.

The narrative is structured in a parallel dual-perspective that gradually unfolds an incredibly bizarre, occult mystery. On one side of the timeline is Hugh Franklin, a socially awkward translator who finds solace in the library. The second timeline introduces Sebastian Everett, an ambitious acquisition specialist whose motivations in regards to the library are entirely transactional. The pairing between the two was a really interesting contrast and kept me fully locked in on the mystery the entire time. I think it's best to go into this one as blind as possible, because it is truly a unique and wild ride. Though classified as a horror novel, there are no jump scares or overly graphic violence. The terror stems from a palpable wrongness embedded in the setting and the creeping dread throughout.

While long-time fans of the creators' previous work will likely spot layers of deeper mythology, I simply found a beautifully written, medium-paced cosmic horror story. I probably shouldn't rate this 5 stars but I couldn't stop reading it, it's such a delicious mix of bibliophilia, skullduggery, and exotic horrors erupting from another universe.

I'm not familiar with Jonathan Sims or the Magnus Archives, but I admire Premee Mohamed's ability to set a tone and deliver some truly shocking horror in a book. This 'secret library' story seems to combine the best features of both authors, so far as I can tell, since Ms. Mohamed is joyfully playing in someone else's sandbox.

Jurgen Leitner is the imperious and coldly distant owner of a secret library that acquires books that are dangerous. These books can warp your mind, or even consume you. The assistants in the library take elaborate precautions to analyze the books' dangers, but sometimes these precautions fail. Mr. Leitner seems to treat the loss of assistants as collateral damage.

There is, however, a spy in his midst. It's no secret to the reader, we see Sebastian's point of view quite early. We also know Sebastian is an entitled git (as they would say in Britain) and probably will get his comeuppance. Unfortunately, others get caught in the web of intrigue. I use the word 'web' intentionally; pay attention to the occurrence of spiders and webs in this book.

There are, of course, others who want these books, and are working to gain access. It all comes to a head in a fantastically lurid account of what happens when books get loose. (Oh come on, you knew that would happen when you heard the basic premise.) The creativity of HOW books are dangerous are a part of what makes this book so much fun.

There is a movie called 'Der Lift' where a sentient elevator kills people, but never the same way twice. You'd be surprised at the number of ways an elevator can kill you. In this book, you'll be impressed with the number of ways a book's subject can determine how it can affect or kill a person. I can only imagine the authors cackling with glee when they came up with another arcane and outrageous method.

For myself? I was creeped out by the first book we see in action, the innocuously titled 'Dig'.

Excuse me, I have to go claw the floor now.

Before anything else: this is billed as a standalone, and technically it is. But I would never recommend it to someone who hasn’t listened to The Magnus Archives, in the same way I’d never recommend The Magnus Protocol before Archives. I’ve read a handful of other reviews, and I’m glad so many people are getting interested in the podcast because of this book. I’m just not sure how much they got out of the story without that context. Nearly every other sentence is a knowing nod to Archives (and Protocol), and skipping them means missing key information.

Hugh/Cobb:
If you know Archives, it’s obvious almost immediately that Hugh is Cobb (which isn’t necessarily a bad thing). The evidence piles up rather quickly, from his childhood, the incident at the museum, the addictive, uncontrollable and compulsive traits like kleptomania and lying, his talent for translation/encoding, and the spider “dream.” It’s all very Web. It reads like a statement Gertrude or Jon would take, or dig up in the archives, right before the statement giver meets their doom. Obviously, the best part of the book was finally getting to watch someone become an avatar from start to finish (besides a select few of the protagonist from Archives, of course). Hugh/Cobb is now my favorite depiction of an avatar of The Web (sorry Ep. 110: Creature Feature & Ep. 136: The Puppeteer).

I think my love for him is also helped by his extremely upsetting past & storyline. He grew up with a family ignoring abuse in his household, because paying attention to anything else hurt less than confronting it. That’s probably why he’s so hellbent on change, cacooning— metamorphosis. That’s why The Mother of Puppet’s embrace, at the end, was seen more to Hugh/Cobb like a pure embrace of love rather than being consumed whole. He could have gotten help as a kid, but he was treated as something to ignore, a problem that would take too long to fix. His family played at being “normal” with what they assumed were good intentions, never saying the quiet part out loud (their disdain for him- a reflection of their fractured family unit) and their coddling was more belittling and dismissive of his pain than anything. It left their youngest son helpless, a groveling mess repeating apologies in a bid for attention or little control over his life and others he wanted to love and be loved by— all when they already thought of him as gross and whiny. Despite this, he still left his family home, sparing them (as best as we can know) and finding more agency in his situation (even if that meant being unhoused (The Web’s arms later becoming his home)), where he could be more of himself (or what he was “becoming.”) Later he used all of it, his emotions of being ignored and having zero to no agency in his life (similar to the trapped books), to manipulate people for the Web’s master plan. That is to say, I really want to give him a hug (he reminds me of Michael, which in itself is a soft spot).

I also loved that even after Hugh/Cobb becomes something “other” (he was frankly already an outcast in society), the grey-and-white spider on Chuck’s sleeve implies he spared her (I’m assuming he pulled some stings and was the one to get her late to work). She was the closest thing to someone who cared about him, and she helped him as best she could under the circumstances. Is she screwed now that the Web has its eyes on her? Definitely. But in my opinion the Web isn’t the worst entity to be marked by, in the grand scheme of things. She’s talented with computers and long-term planning, as well as keeping secrets, so maybe she was already inclined toward it.

Lastly, I also don’t think that Hugh/Cobb is also truly dead after being embraced fully by The Web. I don’t think he’ll be popping up in any more stories, but he would be a fun character to put in a campaign for TMA TTRPG…

Sebastian:
Alright, now for Sebastian. I actually really liked him at first but found his arrogance really annoying in the second half, which I believe is fully intention. He is, up until the end, a cunning, smooth-talking man who will do anything for power (despite already being well-off), even when he doesn’t fully understand the magnitude of the situation he’s in. He thinks the architecture of the house isn’t holding the books back despite being instructed to mess with the house’s layout and each book’s wing by Hugh/Cobb in order to enact the change-in-power (which Sebastian believes is simply a transfer of name and the outcome being him being “all powerful,” not Leitner). Sebastian is literally five steps behind the whole time, which makes for a painful read, considering, if you’ve listened to the show, know this tale in a bloodbath.

I do appreciate the foreshadowing for his choice of death, however, as he is both very Flesh & Stranger coded. Like the Boneturner, Jared Hopworth (weird his friend is also named Sebastian and is a librarian), Sebastian is always striving for more and more, what he perceives as better and better. He’s obsessed with outward appearances, disgusted by Hugh/Cobb (by design),ruled by a sense of toxic masculinity, and always reads people fast so he can use them. He’s constantly around coffee and food (vegetarian), and around/talking about flowers too, which remind me of the garden in season 5 of the show. His body reacts to death (and the horrors he’s committed) while his mind stays numb, and he’d do anything to climb the social ladder (which, again, reminds me of Jared, who instead basically went on to retire from his life of crime). When Sebastian gets to choose a death for a co-worker, he goes straight to the Flesh: big, fast, and gory, just like how he wanted to reach the top as quickly as possible with no regard for human life (despite being one of the only people in good standing with Leitner). So it’s fitting that The Flesh came back to bite him in the ass.

He also nearly had a shot at becoming a Stranger (forgetting himself and his ambitions completely and leaving to act as someone else entirely), but his pride and sense of self got in the way. Even nameless, I was hoping he’d turn it around into something bigger. Obviously, the first handful of seasons demonstrate why The Stranger is a nightmare, but wouldn’t being something be better than nothing at all? He never had enough, though, even when he owned the library and its 900-something books. And that hunger is exactly how Hugh/Cobb got him to stamp every book and symbolically set ownership of everything free. He had Leitner’s trust, and if he’d been patient he probably could have taken the whole place once Leitner was gone (or needed someone to run his hoard effectively). But what good is a museum of someone else’s trash? He obviously liked the thrill of a gory death.

Leitner:
We actually got a little bit more of Leitner in this one. I won’t say that he was my favorite character, but his death was memorable and really set the tone for Elias’ plans. I did like seeing the parallels of Leitner and The Corruption. The strong medicinal smell coming off his breath, the hoarding (a big Corruption theme, especially in Episode 84: Possessive, my favorite Corruption story), and the way he just won’t die, like a cockroach scurrying around underground. How he survived until 2017, I don’t know. If I were Gertrude I would have killed him, but she probably saw him as expendable, someone to squeeze knowledge out of, even if his own hubris would kill him eventually. His fake grave marked with the last name Icarus is clearly what inspired the cover art which is a clever touch. Honestly, he deserved what was coming. If I were Elias, I’d have jumped him just as quickly, Jon or no Jon.

The house:
I was never a fan of the stories about architecture in the original podcast, despite being a lover of art irl. I found each countermeasure in each wing extremely fun, however. I loved The Lonely wing, where everything is forced into position to be seen constantly, and the books are pushed forward and labeled. The Spiral section was another favorite: a scenic walk through the garden where buildings disappear and reappear at random with new doors, all because of Sebastian meddling with the compasses inside. The whole outside section reminded me of the Biltmore Estate in NC. There are lots of allusions to classical mythology, which always makes me think of the Spiral: art, madness, and repeating patterns run through so many of its stories. The Spiral is one of my weaknesses. I feel weirdly parental toward its avatars. I have a soft spot for the Stranger too, and I really liked the bookseller.

What it adds to the main series:
The writing is great, and the book is a lot more on the nose than the podcast. It’s also gorier, since things can be described outright, similar to Jon and Martin’s travels in season 5. The voice acting was amazing (I listened to the audiobook while reading along), and I was very pleased to hear Paul Sims’ voice again for Leitner’s bits. I also liked seeing more of the actual book trade beyond statements and pieces about Gerry and his mom. The books themselves are much more antagonistic and powerful here, and I don’t think I took them as seriously as I should have during the main series. It’ll make relistens of the original show much more dreadful knowing that some books go beyond psychological torture and ghostly summonings.
This book makes Jon receiving “A Guest for Mr. Spider” as a child feel much more diabolical. The Web was already pulling strings, but now there’s the added layer that his guardian was truly targeted on purpose. Maybe someone slipped that book into a stack she bought, or maybe it just spawned there, which is equally terrifying (as a collector myself).

After everything, this story lends an element of the unknown horror back into the podcast. How far does The Web go? Can the books actually be destroyed completely, can we trust that fire would do the job when ripping cannot—given they are actual manifestations (some born and some man-made) of the entities? It also brings into question how much Gertrude and Elias actually knew about the world beyond (in Elias’ case) serving and feeding The Eye with their own hoard of knowledge (or what he presumed to be the ultimate truth).

The classism theme is strong, and the ironic answer at the end is giving power back to the people in a twisted way, by releasing the books back into the world. Maybe they were safer with Leitner, maybe not. By his logic, what are 40-some assistants compared to the countless horrors the books could unleash? He could have destroyed many of them, as Gerry shows in the main series (which I’m now doubting). But would they really be gone? I used to think burning them was a definite yes. Now I’m not so sure, since a book might reform or pop up again, the way Hugh/Cobb’s did after he spilled ink on it and tore it up. Would the bonfire have finished it off? Who knows.

Complaints:
The plot is a bit on the weaker side and I was expecting more. But since the story is literally about the downfall of one of the most pathetic characters in it, I don’t mind. My biggest problem is that the ending feels rushed, as if it’s missing about 75 pages to round things out.

Final thoughts:
I really enjoyed what this adds to the main narrative. It brings more uncertainty about what the entities can do, and another layer of cleverness to the whole thing. I’ll hand it to Elias (begrudgingly), this book made his plans a lot more clever. I’ll be checking out Premee Mohamed’s other work, and reading more of Jonathan’s, which I’m sorely overdue on.

Judging by the blurb, I’m guessing the next book, Work Your Fingers to the Bone, by Christopher Ruz, has to do primarily with The End and The Buried. They’re both very overlooked entities, which makes sense given most people they mark probably die before giving a statement, or become avatars with no reason to visit The Eye’s den in the original series. I really hope we get books centered on all 14 entities. Fingers crossed.

P.S. This book has convinced me even more that Martin should have been a Web (or Corruption) avatar instead of Lonely. Damn you, Peter.
