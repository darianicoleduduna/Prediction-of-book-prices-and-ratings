From the author of The Help.

“You give a girl a taste of fresh air and then you take it away—she’ll grow fierce and wild to get it back.”

Oxford, Mississippi, 1933.

Eleven-year-old Meg Lefleur has learned the hard way to rely on no one.

Ever since her beloved mother failed to come home last Christmas Eve, she’s been one of the “unadoptable” girls at the town’s orphanage, where she fights each day to keep her wits sharp and her spirit unbowed.

When she meets Birdie, a young woman who has come to Oxford determined to remind her socialite sister of the impoverished family she left behind, for the first time in a long while it seems someone else might care about Meg’s future.

But as the Depression tightens its grip, Birdie begins to suspect her sister’s charmed life may be founded on a tapestry of lies. Then, Birdie encounters Charlie, a woman haunted by loss who has been pushed to the brink with nothing left to lose.

Drawn together by circumstance, they find unexpected kinship among a disreputable, determined band of women. But in a town steeped in hypocrisy, even the smallest act of defiance can have dangerous consequences…

Bold, heartwarming, and riotously funny, The Calamity Club is an unforgettable story of resilience and friendship, and a sisterhood of underestimated women who risk everything to take back control of their fates.

Enjoyable, but dragged at times for me.

I picked up The Calamity Club because the premise sounded really appealing: set in 1933 Oxford, Mississippi during the Great Depression, it follows a group of very different women, including a tough young orphan girl named Meg, who form an unlikely sisterhood and team up to take back some control over their lives.

The story has a lot going for it. The friendships that develop feel warm and genuine, with plenty of humor mixed in with the harder moments of poverty, abandonment, and limited options for women at that time. Stockett brings the era to life with vivid details about the orphanage, small-town

Southern life, and the daily struggles everyone faced. I especially liked Meg’s fierce spirit and how the “Calamity Club” idea brings these characters together in unexpected ways. There were parts that made me laugh out loud and others that tugged at the heartstrings.

That said, at around 600 pages, the pacing dragged in the middle for me. A few sections felt repetitive, and some of the plot resolutions wrapped up a little too conveniently. I also wanted a bit more emotional depth from one or two of the supporting characters.

Overall, it’s a solid, entertaining read about resilience and female friendship, but it didn’t quite keep me fully hooked the whole way through.

If you enjoy big-hearted historical novels about strong women banding together during tough times, this one is worth a read- just be prepared for a slower middle section.

It’s 1933 in Oxford Mississippi. Narrated by two characters, Birdie and Meg, the main cast of characters include:

- Meg, an 11 year old girl who lives in an orphanage and considered one of the unadoptable big girls. She’s treated badly but works hard to maintain her bright spirit. I loved her and her story.
- Birdie, considered an “old maid” at 24, arrives in Oxford to ask her sister, Frannie, who married into a wealthy family, for money so she and her mother can pay their taxes and keep their family home. Birdie’s observations about life and her humor were a delight.
- Frannie is a difficult character to like, but as Birdie discovers, her life is far from as it appears on the surface, and things are about to implode
- Mrs Tart, Frannie’s widowed MIL, who has lived a genteel life but will soon need to confront the truth and find a way to survive tough times
- Charlie, a woman down on her luck but whose one mission to find her daughter is her driving force

It’s the Depression, times are tough for everyone, but especially tough for women who are alone in the world. There was no safety net for them, no where to turn. Instead they must rely on their grit and determination to do what it takes, or lose everything.

I loved the theme of strong resilient women even if I couldn’t get on board with HOW they survived tough times.

The author excels at writing well-developed characters, ones you care about deeply. She doesn’t shy away from difficult subjects in our history’s past: the abysmal treatment of orphans, homosexuality, forced sterilization, women’s rights, alcoholism, sex workers, and more.

In fact, I think less is more, and in my view the author perhaps tried to do too much for one book.

And the length of this book! At 656 pages, 250 pages could be cut and the book made better by it. Especially in the beginning, the pages and pages and pages of unnecessary detailed dialogue made it a slog and I considered dnf’ing.

When the plot took a surprising turn around 60%, new characters were introduced and way too much time was spent describing it all in sordid detail.

There’s a great book somewhere among all those pages. Few books are worthy of this length and this isn’t one of them. It was in desperate need of an editor.

For that reason alone I’m giving it 3.5 stars, which, by the way, means I liked it, but there were issues that prevented me from granting it a full 4 stars.

I had this book on my kindle and the audio, and found I much preferred the audio. When I tried to read it I had to fight the urge to skim because I was bored. January LaVoy is one of my very favorite narrators and she excelled, as usual. Her narration brought Birdie to life, and it helped that I could do other things as I listened. Plus I listened at x1.5 - 1.75, depending on which character was narrating, which helped me get through this 29 hour audio.

I said in my review of The Bee Sting that the only books that should be 650 pages are ones that have lots of plot, which is why this doorstopper works. It avoids feeling its length by providing near-constant twists and turns of adversity and comeback.

Back in the day, I was never a big fan of The Help. I know it came from a good place, but I balk a little at the rewriting of history to centre white allies against racism. I can see the value in depicting white people taking a stand against racism, but we also run the risk of whitewashing history when we place white saviours where there were none. For this reason, I preferred the film, where Viola Davis and Octavia Spencer’s dazzling performances kept Skeeter from hogging the emotional centre that should have stayed with the Black women.

I say all this because I think perhaps Stockett took the feedback on board with this novel. While she portrays a diverse cast, she writes from the perspective of two white people— Birdie and Meg —and stays more in her own lane.

There's a lot covered in this plotty pageturner. We begin with Meg living in an orphanage after her mother never came home on Christmas Eve. She lives under the cruelty of Garnett— a bitter woman, active in the Anti-Vice League, who seems to have a particular hatred for Meg. More than anything, Meg wants her mother to come back, but her second best option is to be adopted by a kind family, something which Garnett seems determined to stand in the way of. This whole episode of the story reminded me of one of my most loved (and sobbed over) childhood films: A Little Princess.

Then Birdie comes into Meg's life when she volunteers at The Orphan. This meeting is relatively brief, but it will shape the rest of the story and lead Birdie down a path she never expected when she went to stay with her sister to ask for a loan.

From the devastation caused by the 1929 financial crash, to gay conversion therapy, to prostitution, to section 213 of the 1932 Economy Act that effectively ousted married women from their jobs, to forced sterilization and eugenics, The Calamity Club barely takes a breath. I can't deny its compelling.

Stockett also does a great job of developing her large cast of characters, with even minor characters being fleshed-out and memorable. Just when I was furious at a character, Stockett would put a new spin on it and I’d end up pitying them. Garnett the notable exception.

There were lots of fiery female characters here, heroes and villains alike, while the male characters were characterised by weakness and failure. I actually think one very interesting theme came through with this: the pressure on men to provide and how they crumbled under it. If a person's worth is tied to their financial provision, it is no wonder they suffer in times of economic crash. Many books that centre women are about girl power, and there's no shortage of that in this book, but I also think there's genuine sympathy for the struggling men here.

A deliciously soapy yarn with some necessary content warnings:

This is the type of historical fiction I enjoy most. It's set in 1933, in Mississippi, during the Great Depression. It's about a group of strong women from very different backgrounds. The story is very character-driven, and we get their alternating points of view. Birdie is an "old maid" who goes to Oxford to ask her uppity sister for financial help to pay the taxes on their family home. It doesn't take long for Birdie to figure out her sister's life is nothing but lies. Meg is an eleven-year-old orphan who is very bright but has been treated very badly by the director of the orphanage. Charlie is a woman who feels like she has nothing left to lose. When Birdie meets Charlie, a sort of sisterhood develops with Meg and some of the other townswomen. They want to take back their lives and gain some control again.

I love the entertaining way Stockett deals with heavy social issues. There is a way about her writing that is very atmospheric and emotional, with lots of tension. The women in her stories always have a sense of themselves and are simply trying to get by and get justice. They're poor but have resilience. Again, she shows how Southern society and its politeness is all for show. We get to see the stigma of divorce, homosexuality, and what it means to be an "immoral" woman. Stockett doesn't shy away from the horrific treatment of orphans and women in this one.

The book is over 650 pages long, and I know that seems like a lot to most readers (The Help wasn't a short book at 522 pages), but I devoured it. It is a much slower pace than Stockett's debut, but the characters make it all worthwhile and unforgettable. I felt so much while reading this; I laughed, I teared up, and was oh so very angry. 1930s Mississippi is written about in such great detail that I could feel the humidity and the tension of Oxford. This was worth the seventeen-year wait. Highly recommend!

📅 Mark your calendars: “The Calamity Club” by Kathryn Stockett releases May 5, 2026.
Many thanks to NetGalley and Spiegel & Grau for the early e-copy. All opinions are my own.
Stockett returns to familiar terrain--the American South--to deliver a sweeping and deeply affecting novel about the lives of women navigating survival in post-Depression Mississippi. Set primarily in Oxford, home to the University of Mississippi (Ole Miss), this richly layered story explores themes of racism, misogyny, homophobia, privilege, and even eugenics. As with the best historical fiction, the Author’s Note is essential reading--don’t skip it.

At the heart of the novel is Birdie, a 24-year-old “spinster” living with her mother and grandmother in a struggling Delta town. Facing the loss of their home due to unpaid taxes, Birdie is sent to Oxford to seek help from her younger, wealthier sister, Frances. Frances, married to a banker and living a seemingly comfortable life, offers Birdie a different kind of work instead: helping to untangle the chaotic financial records of a state-run orphanage where she volunteers.

It is there that Birdie meets Meg, an eleven-year-old orphan isolated in a small, moldy room and subjected to the cruelty of the orphanage’s chairwoman. As Birdie spends more time at the institution, she becomes increasingly disturbed by the treatment of the children--and increasingly drawn to Meg’s intelligence, resilience, and quiet strength.

Meanwhile, chaos unfolds on the home front when Frances’s husband absconds with the family fortune, leaving Frances and her mother-in-law in financial ruin. As they set off to track him down, Birdie is left behind to hold things together tasked with saving the family home while navigating a series of unexpected and often precarious circumstances.

Stockett skillfully weaves the narrative through the dual perspectives of Birdie and Meg, expanding the story’s emotional and thematic reach. Along the way, Birdie encounters Charlie, Meg’s mother, and readers are introduced to the chilling realities of the “American Plan”--one of several historical threads that illuminate the harsh and often unjust treatment of women in the Deep South, particularly those who were poor, marginalized, or deemed “undesirable.”

Meg’s storyline, while compelling, does stretch believability at times. She possesses a maturity and emotional depth beyond her years, which may not work for every reader. That said, if you’re willing to embrace a child protagonist who is wise beyond her age, her journey of survival and resilience is deeply rewarding.

There are welcome moments of levity woven throughout, particularly in Birdie’s interactions with a group of determined women working to preserve their homes and dignity. These lighter touches provide balance within an otherwise weighty and, at times, harrowing narrative.

This is not The Help. It is darker, more unflinching, and far more expansive in its examination of systemic injustice and human suffering. A content note for readers sensitive to coarse language: it is present but used authentically and in service of character and setting.

At over 650 pages, this is an ambitious, immersive novel—one that demands your time but rewards it richly. It’s the kind of book you find yourself eager to return to, even as it takes its time unfolding.
📖 A powerful, sweeping work of historical fiction that lingers long after the final page.

To be honest, I probably wouldn’t have picked up this book if it hadn’t been donated to my Little Free Library Shed. But it was difficult to ignore. I enjoyed the author’s “The Help,” and this is her first novel in 17 years, since that bestseller became a global phenomenon.

It wasn’t the premise that gave me pause. I have been reading a great deal about injustice in our world—and, unfortunately, we are witnessing many injustices quite clearly in the United States today under the current administration.

It was the 634 pages that felt daunting. Still, I chose to read it and take my time.

Set in 1930s Mississippi during the Great Depression, the story explores the ways poverty, sexism, class, and institutional power shaped—and often destroyed—the lives of women.

Three women from vastly different socioeconomic backgrounds eventually find their lives intersecting: Birdie, a 24-year-old unmarried bookkeeper struggling financially; Meg, an intelligent 11-year-old abandoned in an asylum for girls; and Charlie, a resourceful woman down on her luck and determined to survive.

As the story unfolds, we see how thoroughly these women have been failed by the legal, marital, economic, and institutional systems of their time. Their choices are limited simply because they are women, and poverty, abandonment, and male authority often have devastating consequences. Yet their ingenuity eventually leads to the creation of the “Calamity Club,” offering the possibility of a way out of poverty.

The book also confronts the patriarchal restrictions placed on women and the disturbing influence of the eugenics movement.

In her author’s note, the author explains that the idea for the novel began with a 1928 Mississippi law allowing the sterilization of people deemed to be “imbeciles.” An all-male board was given the power to determine who fell under that definition, with devastating consequences for disabled people and women.

The historical injustices are among the strongest aspects of the novel. The author does an admirable job immersing readers in the time and place—the oppressive weather, social divisions, and circumstances that left so many women with little control over their own lives.

However, this is a substantial, slow-burn read. The heaviness of the beginning and middle, combined with its deliberate slow pace, may make it feel as though it drags for some readers. Could the same powerful story have been told in fewer pages? For this reader, yes.

Still, for readers willing to settle into its story, there is much to consider about the injustices these women faced—and the strength and ingenuity they found in their efforts to survive.

The Calamity Club is a historical fiction novel set in the heart of Mississippi in the early 1930’s and follows several very strong and “against all odds” female characters. These characters are complicated, oozing with depth, courage and determination.
