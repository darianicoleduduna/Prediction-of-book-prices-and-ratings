About the author

Todd McFarlane

1,937 books475 followers
Todd McFarlane is a Canadian comic book artist, writer, toy manufacturer/designer, and media entrepreneur who is best known as the creator of the epic occult fantasy series Spawn.

In the late 1980s and early 1990s, McFarlane became a comic book superstar due to his work on Marvel Comics' Spider-Man franchise. In 1992, he helped form Image Comics, pulling the occult anti-hero character Spawn from his high school portfolio and updating him for the 1990s. Spawn was one of America's most popular heroes in the 1990's and encouraged a trend in creator-owned comic book properties.

In recent years, McFarlane has illustrated comic books less often, focusing on entrepreneurial efforts, such as McFarlane Toys and Todd McFarlane Entertainment, a film and animation studio.

In September, 2006, it was announced that McFarlane will be the Art Director of the newly formed 38 Studios, formerly Green Monster Games, founded by Curt Schilling.

McFarlane used to be co-owner of National Hockey League's Edmonton Oilers but sold his shares to Daryl Katz. He's also a high-profile collector of history-making baseballs.

1000+ pages of Spawn. That's a lot.

If you're here for the art (is anybody reading this book *not* here for the art?) you'll find some of the best superhero/horror crossover work ever put on a page. Todd McFarlane is a legend for a reason. The dynamic poses, detailed backgrounds, innovative panel layouts, and overwhelming sense of energy and motion bring his pages to life in a way few artists do. And it gets even better when Greg Capullo takes over the pencils – his trademark cartoony-yet-weighty figure drawings and facial expressions pair beautifully with McFarlane's hyperactively detailed inks.

The fill-in issues are a mixed bag. I enjoyed Marc Silvestri's special guest appearance as the penciler for issue 25, showing the characters we've gotten used to through a different, slicker lens. As for Tony Daniel's art... it's not without its high points, but he has a frustrating habit of drawing weirdly misshapen guys that look like piles of laundry. Alternating issues with Capullo doesn't do his work any favors in the comparison.

But what about the story? Overall, I was pleasantly surprised. The first several issues are rough, writing-wise. McFarlane is clearly learning how to write as he goes, and the plotting and dialogue are often awkward and directionless, at least until you get to the first batch of guest writers. He kept at it, though, and by the mid-point of this volume the story is consistently engaging. It also helps that they stopped trying to shoehorn in crossovers with dumb-ass Rob Liefeld characters.

The most consistent theme is questioning power dynamics. All heirarchies (heaven, hell, the police, the military) are shown to be corrupt, self-serving, and to get in the way of even the good-intentioned actors operating within them. And Spawn, badass visual force though he is, invariably makes things worse whenever he defaults to violence as a means of problem-solving. The most heroic actions he takes all involve choosing to give up power to help someone else. This was not the direction I expected from a superhero comic, especially an extreme badass 90s superhero comic, and I was pleasantly surprised.

I have a lot of thoughts regarding this compendium and the Spawn 50 issues it collects. TLDR: 2.5 stars, looks amazing but the story has a lot of problems!

First of all, the artwork does look stellar, nothing in 1993 looked this good. And there is no dip in quality regarding the artwork, whether it's Todd McFarlane or Greg Capullo who pencil the comic.

I often heard beforehand that Spawn looks cool, but the story is a mess. I tend to agree with this statement, as I've found the first ~20 issues to be an almost unreadable mess. Characters ramble a lot and you often have pages full of non-essential text (I'm looking at the three TV reporters which I've started to loathe). But I will admit that the writing does improve and there's a couple of issues here that are actually interesting, from a story perspective.

Onto the next problem: The pacing. Plot points are repeated over and over from one issue to the next with very little progress. Spawn whines about his wife almost every issue, yet nothing happens in this direction. Spawn antagonises the mastermind behind his killing, actually confronts him, the guy survives and Spawn antagonises him again. There's more plot lines just like this in here and it's frustrating.

Another problem I have, is the mapping. They slapped the first 50 issues in here, but there are 3 miniseries referenced throughout the story, with one of them being almost essential to the story; but it's not here!

I think this comic was never meant to be read in big chunks, but can instead be picked up at almost every issue, hence the never ending repetitions in here.

And then the compendium itself. It's so flimsy that the book looks heavily used after just one read through. I have however already read multiple compendiums from DC Comics and they held up amazingly well compared to this edition.

So all in all, I really cannot recommend this, unless you are just into the artwork. Luckily I haven't bought all six compendiums, but stopped after Vol. 2, which I'll dive into next.

3.3/5

Spawn is one of the coolest-looking comics I’ve ever read, which makes it all the more frustrating that I didn’t particularly enjoy reading it. Todd McFarlane’s artwork is absolutely incredible, the character designs are fantastic, and the supernatural world of demons, angels, assassins, and horrifying monsters has so much potential. But across these first 50 issues, I probably only genuinely enjoyed a dozen or two. The biggest problem is surprisingly simple: Spawn doesn’t actually do anything. Or rather, he does plenty of fighting, killing, and sulking, but he rarely seems to want anything or actively work toward a goal. Instead, he spends most of the series reacting to whatever new villain or problem shows up in his alley. It’s a shame, because there’s an awesome story buried somewhere in here.

The premise itself is great. Al Simmons, a murdered government assassin, makes a deal with a demon to return to Earth, only to discover he’s been transformed into a grotesque undead soldier whose very powers are slowly bringing him closer to damnation. The idea that using his abilities to fight evil is also helping build Hell’s army is fascinating, and the politics between Heaven, Hell, and the human organizations responsible for his death could make for an incredible overarching story. Unfortunately, the comic spends far more time teasing these mysteries than actually developing them. Characters like Cagliostro, Angela, Malebolgia, and the Violator appear constantly to deliver cryptic warnings or exposition before disappearing again. Meanwhile, Al returns to his alley to brood until the next threat arrives. There’s a ridiculous amount of narration and repeated backstory, too, which makes an already meandering plot feel even slower. That’s the worst part!

There are definitely some fantastic issues scattered throughout, though. The Overt-Kill battle in #7 is the first time the action really clicked for me, while #8’s exploration of Hell and its soul-trapping horrors introduces some genuinely awesome mythology. The Angela confrontation in #9 is visually spectacular, and the Anti-Spawn storyline in #16–18 is probably my favorite stretch early on in the collection, finally connecting Al’s assassination, government conspiracies, and supernatural enemies into something resembling a larger conflict. Houdini teaching Spawn to manipulate his costume and redirect a nuclear explosion into Hell is another great sequence, while #30’s confrontation with white supremacists is a particularly satisfying showcase of Spawn embracing fear as a weapon. Later, Cy-Gor’s disturbing introduction, Curse dissecting Spawn in #40, and the cloak’s evolution, Black Dispersal against the wanna-be Angel, and fight over Al’s body/soul all deliver the kind of gruesome, imaginative action I desperately wanted more of.

Unfortunately, the characters rarely develop enough to make those moments stick. Al himself is easily the biggest disappointment. He’s understandably tortured by his death and the loss of Wanda, but he spends so much time repeating the same emotional beats that his grief eventually stops feeling meaningful. His decision to suddenly pursue revenge against Terry is especially frustrating after spending so much of the series protecting him. Even Al finally revealing himself to Wanda feels strangely anticlimactic. The supporting cast has similar problems. Sam and Twitch are fantastic, especially Twitch, and I love the idea of them starting their own agency, but they’re frequently sidelined and just sitting in their office or searching alleys. Jason Wynn also spends most of his time sitting in an office, the Violator’s motivations are increasingly convoluted until the end (still shaky), and even potentially fascinating characters like Angela and Cagliostro rarely stick around long enough to meaningfully advance the story.

The best part was the gradual evolution of Spawn’s powers late in the collection. His costume is incredible in design and power, and watching it develop into this increasingly monstrous, spiked, torn-up living organism is awesome. I love how his cape looks too, especially when it’s all alone on the side of a building or something.

Thematically, there’s a lot to work with here, too. Al is caught between Heaven and Hell, constantly questioning whether he’s a monster, a hero, or simply a pawn in someone else’s war. There’s religious horror, government corruption, questions of free will, and a genuinely tragic story about a man unable to let go of the life he lost. It reminds me of Preacher and Hellboy in different ways, with the provocative religious mythology of the first and the cryptic, apocalyptic worldbuilding of the second. Unfortunately, it also shares some of the storytelling problems I had with both. The world is often more interesting than the actual plot, and the constant hints at some enormous future confrontation rarely translate into satisfying progression in the present.

The ending does offer a little hope. Al curing Terry’s illness and traveling to Hell is a nice cliffhanger. Hopefully the series is finally building toward something larger. After 50 issues, I’m hesitant to get too excited about the next book.

Overall, Spawn was a pretty disappointing read, despite having some amazing American comic artwork. The designs are incredible, the world is fascinating, and there are occasional bursts of fantastic horror and action, but the storytelling is far too repetitive and directionless for me to love it. Al Simmons has all the makings of an incredible antihero, yet he spends far too much time being a passenger in his own story. I desperately hope Spawn finally gives its incredible central character an actual story worth telling.
