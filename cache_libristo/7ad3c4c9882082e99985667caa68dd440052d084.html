Because I Killed Him

Edith Birde

4.43

Rate this book
Guillotines made of gold still cut off heads.

In the Civilized World, everyone is rich, yet money doesn’t buy safety. It only buys a gold coffin. The real currency is having the right allies. Low-citizens survive by joining high-citizens’ exclusive inner circles, trading loyalty for protection from laws that govern speech, dress, and posture—and punish mistakes with public beheading.

Low-citizen Loredana Waldsten already knows the cost of breaking the rules. Once a rising fencing prodigy, she lost the right to carry weapons after killing a high-citizen in a brutal locker-room attack. The courts erased his death to preserve his family’s honor. Now she’s unarmed, legally defenseless, and enrolled at the elite Grandmaster University, where champagne spills into the gutters and reputations are built on death duels.

When Loredana’s father, a low-citizen politician, publicly challenges the high-citizens, she becomes a target. Some classmates demand her execution. Others hunt her for sport. And by law, she’s forbidden to fight back.

Her only chance of survival lies with Edmund Prew, a charming yet ruthless high-citizen student she’s been warned against. Edmund's family has been locked in a bitter feud with Loredana’s for years, and he wants nothing to do with her until a lost bet forces him to protect her within his inner circle. What begins as a scandalous, strategic alliance turns perilous as they fall for each other.

Because the man Loredana killed was more than an average high-citizen. And if Edmund ever learns the truth, the only person keeping Loredana alive may become the one most determined to kill her.

I read this on a bit of a whim because I was looking for something light and propulsive, and this one is astonishingly popular on Goodreads. Even more astonishingly so after finishing it, because I just don't get it. I mean, I get some of it. But I’m surprised this many people made it through all 600 pages, never mind actually loved it enough to rate 5 stars.

It starts okay. I was getting late 2000s-early 2010s dystopian fantasy vibes— enjoyable enough, even if the world didn't make a whole lot of sense. But a combination of poor pacing and just an overload of ill-conceived concepts quickly made it a slog.

Because I Killed Him reads like someone made a list of random elements they thought might be good in a book, and then just mashed them all together regardless of whether they worked or not. There's so many things going on in this book that are neither adequately explained nor cohesive — coloured bloods, fencing, heretic movement, Bliss drug, pregnancy is illegal, hovercars, genetic engineering, babies grown in labs, and robot servants called "pinkies".

So much doesn't quite add up. Blue Bloods are high citizens, preferred for their superior blood, while Greens (like Loredana) and others are low citizens. Pregnancy is also illegal and babies are grown in labs... so why keep making low citizens? The eugenics concept was not well thought-out.

The pacing was also off. There were occasional good scenes, but much of it dragged. I frequently felt like we were meandering along without the action or emotion elevating. The whole plot is basically Loredana evading the fallout of her dad’s political actions, yet she never actually seemed to be in danger. There was very little suspense.

Equally, the romance is absent until around 60% and then it is dialled straight up to madly in love. This is not the kind of love story I have it in me to care about. Also, a side character calling women “broad” made me want to murder someone.

A lot was left to be desired, in my opinion. A competent editor would have got this down to half the size, improving the pacing and shaving off a lot of waffle in the world-building that didn't make sense anyway.

It tootles along, failing to build up a climax until we get to the very end where it drops off at a cliffhanger. And the ending is so abrupt that it literally felt like an unfinished scene.

Truly one of the best books I’ve read this year.

And honestly? I did not expect that going in.

This is not my usual genre, and when I first saw that it was a dystopian story set in a 1920s-inspired futuristic world with flying cars, I hesitated. It sounded like a lot for my brain to process. But wow. Just…wow.

This is one of those books that completely consumes you. The kind where you stop feeling like you’re reading and start feeling like you’re living inside the pages. The writing is so immersive and beautifully done that I could see every scene, feel every moment of tension, and completely lose myself in this world.

The class system. The inequality. The secrets. The forbidden romance. The constant feeling that there was something much bigger happening beneath the surface. Every piece of this story pulled me deeper and deeper.

One of my favorite things about dystopian stories is watching people who have been pushed down for generations finally realize their own power, and this book absolutely delivered on that.

At its core, this is a story about oppression, control, identity, and what happens when people who have been silenced decide they are done being obedient. The society in this book was fascinating and heartbreaking because of how intentionally it was built to separate, categorize, and control its citizens.

The systems of power, the manufactured hierarchy, and the way people are taught to accept their place in the world made the fight against it feel even more powerful. There is something so satisfying about watching characters who have been told they are lesser, disposable, or unworthy begin to question everything they’ve been taught and fight for their freedom.

The tension wasn’t just about survival... it was about reclaiming identity, choice, and the right to decide your own future.

Loredana, my girl.
She is stubborn. Prideful. Fiercely determined. But underneath all of that is someone who is lost, trying to find her place in a world that has already decided her worth before she ever had a chance.
Watching her navigate a society that keeps her at the bottom because of the color she bleeds, while slowly uncovering the truth around her and growing into her own strength, was incredible.

I will always love a strong FMC, but what I loved about Loredana was that her strength wasn’t just about being fearless. It was about surviving. Questioning. Growing. Choosing herself even when the world told her she shouldn’t.

And Edmund Prew…
The man that you are.

Dark. Mysterious. Complicated. The kind of character who makes you want to uncover every scar, every secret, and every piece of his past that shaped him.

But beneath that intimidating exterior is someone with so much loyalty and heart for the people he chooses to protect. Edmund is not someone who gives trust easily, but when you earn it? You have it completely.

And when he loves?
He LOVES.

Despite bleeding two different colors in a society that has literally engineered and categorized its citizens from birth, the connection between Edmund and Loredana felt so painfully human.
Their love wasn’t just forbidden because of who they were — it was forbidden because the world they lived in was built to keep people like them apart.

Watching them choose each other again and again was devastatingly beautiful. Their devotion, their trust, and the way they see each other beyond the labels forced onto them was everything.

The side characters and found family were also something I didn’t know this story needed until I had it. They brought so much warmth, humor, and depth to a world that could have easily felt heavy. The mystery, the drama, the relationships, and the twists all worked together so beautifully.

And that ending???

I am spiraling.

The last 40% of this book had me wanting to tear my hair out. Every assumption I made? Destroyed. Every theory I thought I had figured out? Questioned.

The buildup felt like being strapped into a rollercoaster knowing the drop was coming, but still not being prepared for the free fall.

I finished this book and immediately entered the “I need everyone I know to read this so I have someone to scream with about my theories” stage.

I genuinely do not know what to do with myself besides beg anyone and everyone to pick this book up so I am no longer alone in my obsession.

A stunning world. Unforgettable characters. A devastatingly beautiful romance. A rebellion worth rooting for. And an ending that will haunt me until I get answers.

Please read this so I have someone to spiral with!!

Oh how I hate this book. It started off somewhat strong, but in truth, I was waiting until half way through for the book to get good and then it completely fell apart. The unravelling started post Loredana and Edmund coupling up and completely fell apart circa monkey shit eating.

A lot of the dystopian world building gave me Divergent vibes, if only a bit better. While I appreciate that the authors didn’t want to give you all the exposition right at the start, what it ended up being was completely nonsensical. Also the culture of the world felt so under developed, I could never believe it. 1920’s culture with a sudden injection of futuristic elements. It doesn’t make sense in the world of the book, or even in practice. What do you mean they fight with graphene sabers? Why is everyone carrying a weapon? Why are Death Duels even a thing? Also, given totalitarian the government is, you’d assume with all these technological advancements that all Bonds would be monitored, to catch Heritics and Vulgars.

This also needed serious editing. A lot of the descriptions don’t make sense at all, not to mention all my issues with the plot. For example, during the surfing scene with the whole gang, Loredana is gripping Ed around the neck while he’s paddling with his arms and her feet are flying behind her. But also in the next sentence, his feet are planted as he grabs the rails, the next he quickly switches them around and she’s crouched in front of him. What?! Is this a jet ski? Is this a surfboard?? When I got to the end and saw that the book was written by two people, that made the source of a lot of the inconsistencies much clearer. Also, How are they kissing on a galloping horse whose top speed is 100 mph? Hello??!!!

In no particular order, my gripes with the book are:

1. Vulgars are illegal and will be executed, by what differentiates a Vulgar from what these two are doing? Is it just sex? Is it trying to have a baby?

2. How are so many people being executed on a daily basis? Whenever it’s mentioned, there’s always more than one, so are more than 500 students being killed every year? Given that they’re a small portion of the world, and how rigid their baby making system is, how are they not extinct by now after 200 years of this?

3. What is the plot? Is it just Lore trying to stay alive? Is that it? To me, the whole story was so disjointed where it went from Academia Romance Drama to Dystopian Hellscape to Politics Whatever.

4. Entourages. Why? I truly don’t understand why any Blue would choose to have an entourage, outside of Edmund. Like why does Lily want a group of low-citizens just following her around? What does she truly gain out of protecting them? The initial point of they ask for sexual services also falls apart once the concept of Vulgars is introduced.

5. The whole friendship with Charlotte was actually the first whiplash. Lore went from not even wanting to look at her to forgiving her in an instant. Charlotte’s justification truly didn’t make sense to me. The second was the whole Edmund third act behavior. You mean he gives her Bliss, removes her from his entourage (which is what enabled the whole credit drain thing anyway), and insults her family and her father and we just forgive that in a instant? because he did it to protect her? I call bullshit.

6. The shit eating scene. What the actual fuck was that? We’ve established Rosamund is crazy (why? we don’t fully know beyond abuse and drugs. But her obsession with Edmund and Jack, your guess is as good as mine), so her asking Lore to eat shit so she would let go of Charlotte. In what world would Rosamund actually let go following the shit eating? News flash, she doesn’t here either. Henry pulls her off of Charlotte. This scene nearly made me DNF, but the end was in sight.

7. Loredana actually has no regard for consequence. Giving her energy shield to Croft? Revealing to everyone that her weapons restriction is lifted? Dropping her earring and making no attempt to retrieve it before answering Hillaire’s call (she says she only calls when it can’t wait, but she didn’t end up talking to her dad until a month later anyway)? Taking the Brasscoat offer? She could’ve just kept the Aegis until the section 27 thing was resolved as Jerome offered but she decided to immediately jump into the Brasscoat plot, which makes no sense! She has to go back to lying to everyone right after all her lies were lifted off her.

8. Edmund’s scratches. Why was it ever assumed that a mistress was clawing at him so severely she would draw blood all over his face. From his first scene, it made no sense to me that who they assumed to be Irene was drawing all that blood. In what world does a sexual, non abusive encounter do that? His relationship with his mother also doesn’t make sense to me, where he says he takes the abuse because he can’t see her blood spilt or whatever. I totally and completely understand him not fighting back against his mother, but the haughty way he speaks to her when Lore is spying then taking a complete 180 seconds later. His initial haughtiness and confidence don’t mesh with his discipline in the face of the abuse. Also, does his mother have claws???? How is she flaying his skin, and cutting him down to the bone?

9. The sequel bait ending.

I have so much more to say and so many more problems with this book. I genuinely can’t understand why anyone would rate this higher than 3 stars. My 2 stars feel generous in all honesty. I’m only giving it that much because the writing wasn’t bad, just in desperate need of editing and oversight, and for the hope I had for the first half of this book. The yearning got me, but that quickly fell short when it turned into a romantic montage only one chapter later.

How in the world am I supposed to pick up another book after this one? When I say this story consumed me, I mean I almost read 500 pages in a single day because I physically could not stop.

There’s so much to unpack here, but I’m going to try.

This world gave very strong Great Gatsby vibes. Everyone is expected to be polished, proper, and painfully careful with their words in public. Formal introductions. Perfect posture. Perfect appearances. But behind closed doors, inside their salons and inner circles, people finally speak like real humans. That contrast added so much tension to the story because every interaction felt dangerous.

The story follows Loredana Waldsten, a green low-citizen who already knows exactly what it costs to break the rules after killing a blue high-citizen during a brutal attack years before. Now she’s been stripped of the right to carry weapons, legally forbidden from defending herself, and forced into the elite Grandmaster University where reputations are built on death duels, power, and survival.

From the very beginning, you’re thrown into this massive world where Bloody Sundays exist. Citizens who lose their civil credits are publicly executed, and somehow it’s always the low-citizens paying the price while the high-citizens walk away untouched. There’s also a drug called Bliss woven into the political conflict, and Loredana’s father, a politician fighting to ban it, sparks controversy that puts an even larger target on her back at the university.

Then Edmund Prew enters the story, and EVERYTHING changes.

Edmund comes from one of the powerful high-citizen families, and his family has hated Loredana’s for years. After losing a bet, he’s forced to bring her into his inner circle and protect her, even though being associated with her could destroy him socially. What starts as this strategic alliance slowly turns into something so much deeper, filled with tension, lingering looks, soft touches, and the kind of yearning that actually makes your chest hurt.

What makes their relationship even more dangerous is that Loredana and Edmund are never supposed to be together in the first place. She’s a Green, he’s a Blue, and in their world those lines are not meant to be crossed. Relationships between classes are seen as scandalous, illegal, and a threat to the social order they’ve built everything around. If they’re caught, the consequences aren’t just whispers and ruined reputations. They could literally face the guillotine. So every stolen touch, every secret meeting, and every moment between them feels forbidden in the most terrifying way possible

I’m not kidding when I say I will never look at seashells, mud, or daffodils the same way again. Edmund was written so beautifully it honestly hurt. This man was written by women in the best possible way, and somehow Edith Birde managed to make him feel both impossibly soft and dangerously sharp at the same time. He permanently stamped a piece of this story onto my soul.

The found family in this book was incredible too. The side characters felt real, layered, and deeply important to the story. I adored Dickie and genuinely wish I could be friends with him in real life. And Charlotte… her relationship with Loredana meant so much to me. That scene toward the end absolutely wrecked me once you fully understand what their friendship truly represents.

This book balances worldbuilding, politics, class division, social tension, and brutal survival so well while still delivering a romance that completely CONSUMED me. Every piece worked together perfectly. Every moment between them feels like a ticking time bomb waiting to explode.

Everything felt so poetic and vivid that it played like a movie in my head the entire time I was reading. The descriptions, the tension, the emotions, the dialogue… all of it felt so intentional and immersive. This is not a story you simply read and move on from. This is one that crawls under your skin and stays there long after the final page.
