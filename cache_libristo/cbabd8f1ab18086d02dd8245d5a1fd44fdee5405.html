In a near-future world, humans and non-humans alike must endure in a world that wants them dead. The fan-favorite danmei (Boys’ Love) novels return with an all-new translation and illustrations!

In the year 2020, humanity was nearly wiped out by cosmic radiation. Over the next one hundred years, living creatures began to change and mutate while the remaining humans struggled to survive in man-made bases.

Down in the Abyss, a home to mutated xenogenics, there lived a sentient mushroom. Nourished by the flesh and blood of the deceased An Ze, the mushroom took on the form of its host and even adopted a similar name: An Zhe. Seeking his missing spore, harvested by humans, An Zhe is determined to make it to the human base to retrieve what’s his. But the closer he gets to the base, the higher his risk of death increases.

Unfortunately for An Zhe, there are those who prowl the streets seeking xenogenics like himself—known as Judges, they inspect and eliminate non-humans without a second thought, sparing no one. And when An Zhe finds himself under the scrutiny of Colonel Lu Feng—the most perceptive and merciless of the Judges—he somehow goes undetected but not unsuspected. Lu Feng knows there’s something off about An Zhe, and he’s not letting him out of his sight until he figures it out.

Can An Zhe get back his spore without revealing his mutation to Lu Feng, or will everything unravel?

I inhaled all 27 chapters of this book in something of a daze and I am now a changed person.

Little Mushroom: Judgment Day, the 1st book in the Little Mushroom duology, follows the story of a sentient little mushroom that gains human form and journeys undetected through a dystopian world in search for his stolen spore—and into the path of the one human who can discover the truth of what he is and kill him for it.

If this sounds unlike anything you’ve ever read—it’s probably because it is. Shisi offers a fearlessly inventive and deliciously bonkers spin on the post-apocalyptic novel that is also downright endearing and a lot of fun along the way. But don’t let the cute exterior fool you—Little Mushroom: Judgment Day packs a hefty punch, achieving more levels of complexity, imagination, and depth than a surface-reading might give it credit for.

This is a story about what it means to survive in a world that perpetually flickers towards extinction. It asks questions central to our very species: what does it do to a person to understand, acutely and with pain, the world’s capacity for violence? Who are we after enduring, when all that’s left of us is so ruthless to survive even when we don’t have the stomach for it?

Dystopia is about things carried to extremes, and Shisi is unafraid to push past the hold of even our most uncomfortable imaginings. The novel’s resounding theme—should human interests be put above all else?—haunts the pages, reminding us that we are all-too-often sustained and consoled by the knowledge that science will always prevail, that survival is inevitable, that collapse is something that happens to other people in far away places. Or that, at the very least, we can always reassemble ourselves in the aftermath of collapse.

It is against this monumental reality of cataclysm that An Zhe’s innocent search for his spore unfolds, and because he’s a literal mushroom, with a rudimentary and naive understanding of good and evil or of what propels humans to do the things they do, there is very little space for the endless self-justifying impulse of “human interest.” In a little mushroom’s rendering of the world, the ugliness—that is to say, all the details of struggle and survival—is stark and unflinching, with nothing to hide behind. What we are ultimately left with is a haunted and haunting portrait of what we do to one another in our desperate grasping to unmake our undoing.

Oh, and this is also a queer love story!

All in all, I’m grateful for the enthusiasm of the several people who suggested this book to me. I signed up for a whipcrack sci-fi romp and got much more than I bargained for—and my reading experience was definitely all the better for it.

*Ich habe ein kostenloses Rezensionsexemplar erhalten und gebe diese Rezension freiwillig ab.*

Lese-Empfehlung

Little Mushroom ist eine dystopische New-Adult-Boys-Love-Light-Novel. Tödliche Xenogene lauern unter den Menschen und es ist die Aufgabe der Richter das Überleben der Menschheit zu sichern. Lu Feng ist ein gnadenloser Richter, der jeden Xenogen tötet, der ihm begegnet. An Zhe hingegen ist ein Pilz-Xenogen der die Stadtmauern überwinden muss um seine verlorenen Sporen zurückzugewinnen. Zwischen Xenogen und Richter beginnt ein gefährliches Katz-und-Maus-Spiel das sich zu einem tödlichen Überlebenskampf entwickelt. Wer wird am Ende die Oberhand gewinnen, die Xenogene oder die Menschheit?

Während das Original ausschließlich als Novel erschienen ist, bietet die deutsche Erstausgabe einen prächtigen Farbschnitt sowie Innenillustrationen. Die Geschichte verbindet Romanze, Dystopie und Science-Fiction zu einer Erzählung über Überleben, Identität und Menschlichkeit.

Das Erzähltempo ist gemächlich und wird von umfangreichem Weltbau, ideologischen Auseinandersetzungen, biologisch-wissenschaftlichen Erklärungen und einer faschistischen Regierungsmacht geprägt. Die dystopischen und Science-Fiction-Elemente stehen dabei stärker im Vordergrund als die romantische Beziehung.

Einleitung

Vor einiger Zeit gab es ein Mädchen in meinem Kurs, das laut aufschrie als eine Biene in den Raum flog. Zunächst dachte ich, sie ist eine von vielen, die Bienen nicht mögen. Jedoch stellte es sich heraus, dass ihre Panik tiefgründiger war. Ein einziger Stich könnte für sie tödlich sein—sie reagiert allergisch auf Bienengift.

Seitdem frage ich mich, wie gefährlich Insekten tatsächlich sind. Während manche Menschen ohne Allergien oder Krankheiten aufwachsen, kann für andere ein einziger Insektenstich lebensbedrohlich sein. Insekten haben eine hohe Krankheitsübertragung—sie sind die gefährlichsten Tiere der Welt für Menschen. Ohne die Immunität eines Großteils der Menschheit wäre eine Apokalypse schon längst ausgebrochen—ähnlich wie in der Welt von Little Mushroom.

Schreibstil

Übersetzung
Besonders positiv hervorzuheben ist die Arbeit der Übersetzerin Sarah Ozolnieks. Es ist bemerkenswert wie viel Mühe sie sich gibt, das deutsche Leseerlebnis dem chinesischen Original möglichst nahe zu bringen. Auch die Fußnoten sind hilfreich, insbesondere der chinesischen Namen, ihrer korrekten Schreibweise und Aussprache. Insgesamt trägt die sorgfältige Übersetzung zu einem gelungenen Leseerlebnis bei.

Emotionale Distanz
Beide Protagonisten sind emotional distanziert. Lu Feng ist gefühlskalt und abweisend. An Zhe ist ein Betrachter der Geschehnisse und dadurch meist passiv. Der Schreibstil ist durch eine Erzählperspektive in der dritten Person geprägt, wodurch man nur selten Einblicke in die Gefühlswelt der Protagonisten erhält. Hauptsächlich werden An Zhes Gedanken und Gefühle gezeigt, während Lu Fengs Innenleben unklar bleibt. Eine ausführlichere Darstellung der Gedanken und Gefühle beider Protagonisten hätte es erleichtert, sich stärker in sie hineinzuversetzen. Auch eine subtilere Charakterisierung hätte dazu beitragen können, Lu Feng besser zu verstehen und emotional stärker von seiner romantischen Beziehung zu An Zhe berührt zu werden.

Weltbau
Little Mushroom bietet umfangreiche Hintergrundinformationen zur apokalyptischen Welt, ihren Ideologien und der gegenwärtigen faschistischen Herrschaft. Auch die Erklärungen zu Genmutationen und weiteren Science-Fiction-Elementen sind detailliert und verleihen der Welt zusätzliche Tiefe. Insgesamt ist der Weltbau gut gelungen.

Themen

Themen wie Menschlichkeit, Überleben, wissenschaftliche Entdeckungen, zwischenmenschliche Verbindungen und Identität prägen Little Mushroom. Sie binden sich in den Weltbau und deren Charakteren ein. Im Laufe der Handlungsstränge, beginnt man sich als Leser zu fragen was Menschsein tatsächlich bedeutet. Bestimmt die Genetik oder das Verhalten darüber wer Mensch und wer Xenogen ist? Der Pilz-Xenogen, An Zhe, beweist, dass Empathie und Freundlichkeit keine Eigenschaften sind die ausschließlich Menschen kennzeichnen, sondern auch bei Xenogenen zu finden sind. Die gesellschaftlichen Konflikte und ideologischen Auseinandersetzungen hinterfragen die faschistische Herrschaft und die bestehende soziale Hierarchie. Menschen werden anhand ihres gemeinschaftlichen Nutzens gnadenlos aussortiert um das kollektive „größere Wohl” zu gewährleisten. Der militärische Slogan „Das Interesse der Menschheit steht über allem” meint dabei nicht die Rechte des Individuums sondern vielmehr die natürliche Auslese und die Selektion durch das Militär.

Charaktere

Leider waren die Charaktere durch ihrer emotionalen Distanz weniger ansprechend als beispielsweise der ausführliche Weltbau. An Zhe ist sanftmütig. Als Xenogen nimmt er die Geschehnisse aus einer beobachtenden Perspektive wahr. Ich hatte mir erhofft, dass er im Verlauf der Handlungsstränge mehr aktiv in das Geschehen eingreifen würde. Jedoch bleiben seine Handlungen auf die Erfüllung seiner eigenen Interessen beschränkt. Ironischerweise erinnert dieses Verhalten an das eines Menschen, der sich nur selten für seine Mitmenschen einsetzt.

Lu Feng verhält sich ähnlich. Allerdings vermute ich, dass seine Gefühlskälte eine emotionale Barriere darstellt, die ihn davor bewahren soll dem Wahnsinn zu zerfallen. Beide Protagonisten habe nachvollziehbare Gründe für ihre emotionale Zurückhaltung. Trotzdem erschwert mir diese Distanz die emotionale Bindung zu ihnen, insbesondere bei der Romanze. Die Kommunikation zwischen Lu Feng und An Zhe bleibt meist kurz und zurückhaltend. Häufig begegnen sie sich ohne viele Worte miteinander zu wechseln. Ihre Beziehung ist meist still und ruhig. Es ist nicht das, was ich mir von einer Geschichte über die erste Liebe erwarte.

Fazit

Little Mushroom ist eine gelungene dystopische Science-Fiction-Light-Novel. Weniger gelungen sind hingegen die Charaktergestaltung und die Romanze. Abgesehen von An Zhe und Lu Feng bleiben die restlichen Charaktere wenig erinnerungswürdig. Auch die emotionale Distanz der Protagonisten erschwert für mich den emotionalen Zugang zu ihrer romantischen Beziehung. Den zweiten Band werde ich definitiv lesen, allerdings nicht wegen der Romanze (die sich für mich kaum wie eine anfühlt) sondern wegen des gelungenen Weltbaus sowie der Science-Fiction- und Dystopie-Elemente.

Okay, I have no idea what to write for this book because, when I love something this much and give it 5 stars, it's usually just me over-rambling LOL.

There are a few books that make it difficult to muster the words, simply because so many thoughts and feelings knot together inside me. I find myself searching for a single sentence that might capture even a fraction of what the story left behind, while knowing no review could ever satisfy the ache it created. I didn't expect Little Mushroom to settle so deeply into the back of my mind, typed onto the pages of my memory like a long-buried journal entry. Sci-fi has always been a dubious genre for me, yet there was a strange beauty to this story that I couldn't stop following its journey to the very end. It is cold, damp, and lingeringly tactile, like stepping outside after a torrential downpour to find the air still bruised and smelling faintly of ozone.

Some spoilers beyond this point.

The world isn't merely post-apocalyptic; it is physically hostile. Humanity has been reduced to scattered enclaves behind towering concrete monoliths, besieged by cosmic radiation and a mutated ecosystem where super-bacteria, fungi, and viruses cause rapid inter-species genetic assimilation. Inside the sectors, life is sterile, crowded, and severely controlled. Outside, everything is wet, dark, ravenous, and alive. Human civilization feels like a flickering candle in a vast, black tide, and the wilderness is slowly swallowing the flame.

In a world where human values have been discarded or corrupted, An Zhe moves through the ruins with an eerie, unhurried stillness. Rather than an alien entity, he is like a non-human vessel awakening to a world he was never built to comprehend; he enters the narrative without a single human premise. Beginning as a simple fungal organism in the Abyss who absorbs the genes and identity of a dying human youth, he gathers the weight of existence piece by piece through impression and loss. His imperatives are small and ancient: he wants moisture, he wants somewhere safe to take root, and he wants his stolen spore back. He does not comprehend military hierarchy, social status, or the brutal moral calculus humans constructed to justify their survival. When navigating human gene testing, he experiences genuine confusion simply because mushrooms are neither animals nor plants—he is merely a "law-abiding mushroom" trying to navigate human rules. He observes humanity with the same detached wonder with which an ancient forest might watch a passing storm.

Because he remains untainted by human ideology, An Zhe is the only soul in the story completely unburdened by its prejudices. Where humans look out at the wilderness and see monsters, An Zhe sees a balanced ecosystem. Where they see infected threats that must be eradicated, he sees living creatures merely trying to endure. He does not understand why some lives are deemed worthy of salvation and others disposable.

In a world where survival has reduced existence to pure accounting, where bodies are fuel, people are metrics, and worth is tied entirely to function, An Zhe simply refuses to participate in the math. His innocence isn't naive; it functions as a form of radical resistance. By gathering impressions of human suffering without absorbing their cruelty, his gaze strips away our self-justifications, exposing how easily institutionalized violence becomes the default setting for survival.

This truth surfaces with heartbreaking monologue during his final moments with the real An Ze. As he lies dying in the wild, An Ze whispers: "I'm someone whose life had no meaning... I'm very happy to not go back to the human base. Just like the wilderness, they're both... places where only people with value can live on." That sense of relief, at no longer having to answer to a society where the right to exist was conditional on utility is devastating. When An Ze recognizes the little mushroom as something gentle, it forces a terrible question: how broken must a world become for simple gentleness to feel ourwardly special? An Zhe never truly becomes human over the course of the story. If anything, his non-human clarity makes us reconsider what being human was supposed to mean in the first place.

If An Zhe embodies everything this world has tried to eradicate; softness, vulnerability, instinct.... then Lu Feng is the weapon forged to take its place. He is steel because someone decided steel was the only material that wouldn't shatter. As the Arbiter of the Trial Court, he bears authority over life and death, holding the unbearable burden of deciding who lives and who is executed before contamination can consume the enclave. Painted across the base is the uncompromising truth of their existence: "Humankind's interests take precedence over all else." Sometimes saving a thousand lives really does require pulling the trigger on one. Compassion, in his position, is a luxury that costs hundreds of deaths.

That is the cruelest tragedy of Lu Feng: his detachment isn't a natural absence of feeling, but a frost weathered into his skin through every trigger pulled, a duty performed so relentlessly that the mask has long since fused to the bone. He has been reduced to a function so bitterly that even his humanity becomes secondary to what he can endure, decide, and sacrifice. Forced to execute his own mother after she contracts contamination in the wild, he kills the woman who gave him life because the cold mathematics of survival demand it. He lives as a man carved down to a singular function, carrying a question he rarely voices aloud: "I'll just sometimes think about... the choices I made. Just what am I judging, and who will judge me in the end?" Even his living quarters mirror this internal isolation: an unyielding, sterile sanctuary of glass and iron where no warmth is invited, and nothing soft is permitted to find purchase.

Which makes An Zhe's presence in that hollow room feel almost startlingly alive.

Their dynamic resonates so deeply because both survive at the very fringes of human society. Lu Feng has been forced to become inhuman so that humanity might endure. An Zhe is literally non-human, yet he is the only creature capable of looking at Lu Feng without demanding a justification for his existence. An Zhe doesn't offer moral forgiveness or fairy-tale redemption. He doesn't ask Lu Feng to put down his gun or ignore the world's cruelty. He simply gives him a corner of existence where he is allowed to stop acting as a weapon.

Their intimacy lives in gestures so subtle they'd go unnoticed anywhere else, yet here, in a world stripped of softness, they loom vast: Lu Feng shielding An Zhe's eyes from the blinding white flash of a bombing raid; wiping blood from An Zhe's cheek with a thumb; patiently fixing clumsy bandages when An Zhe hurts himself; allowing the mushroom to remain beside him when every rule of the human world demands its destruction. And An Zhe, lying awake in the darkness of the Arbiter's quarters, understands the contradiction of the room instinctively: sleeping beside Lu Feng feels "very dangerous, yet also very safe."

Lu Feng is a terror to almost everyone alive, yet he becomes An Zhe's shelter. An Zhe is an anomaly that should be hunted down and incinerated, yet he becomes the only gaze that looks at Lu Feng without calculating his cost. Even An Zhe's retrieved spore, flattening itself into a small white disc against the lab glass just to press toward him, instinctively craves Lu Feng's touch. In a world where everyone is valued for what they can do, these small moments of care mean everything.

Instead, they grant each other an exemption from the world's unrelenting demands. Beside one another, An Zhe is no longer a threat to be purged, and Lu Feng is no longer an instrument to be wielded. For brief stretches of time, the roles imposed on them simply fall away.

And perhaps that is what hurts most about Lu Feng: after spending his entire life becoming what humanity needed him to be, he finally finds someone who asks nothing of him. An Zhe does not love the Arbiter, the executioner, or the weapon. He simply stays beside Lu Feng. For someone who has spent his life turning himself into a shield for everyone else, having someone remain beside him without asking him to be anything in return may be the closest thing to tenderness he has ever known

For all its focus on survival, Little Mushroom is less about surviving the end of the world and more about what the effort to survive does to the human soul. When every mistake carries a death toll, morality shrinks to brutal utility. People become categories, mercy becomes a hazard, and love feels like an indulgence. The novel never pretends these hard choices are unnecessary, but it asks what is left of us when utility is the only value remaining. What happens to the things that cannot be justified by survival alone, gentleness, vulnerability, affection, and the simple right to exist without having to earn your breath?

An Zhe embodies those fragile things without needing to understand them. Lu Feng understands their worth entirely, having spent his life stripped of their comfort. That is why they fit together, a bit painfully and sweet. One is a mushroom holding onto an Arbiter's silver badge, waiting before he can return to his cave in the Abyss. The other is a man who has sacrificed everything he loved to buy one more day for a dying species. And there, at the sharp edge of extinction, they find the one thing neither was ever supposed to have: a reason to be gentle.

Little Mushroom does not claim humanity is precious because we are good. It makes humanity precious because even when the world demands cruelty, a soul can still choose not to surrender every tender part of itself. And perhaps that is the most beautiful truth a story about our end could ever tell.

3 little mushrooms

"Humankind's interests take precedence over all else. It's not kindness to a single person, it's kindness to the fate of all of humankind. This is the source of unwavering conviction"

First of all, the concept is really original and captivating. This cute little mushroom has taken on human form and is now exploring this post-apocalyptic planet to discover the purpose of life and how people adapt to survive.

This is entirely my fault; although I'm not a huge lover of science fiction, I wanted to give it a shot because I adore Damei and it had a great rating. Nevertheless, I wasn't happy with the outcome.

I'm relieved that the pace is quick and simple to follow because I was feeling disconnected at times. I just couldn't get into this book.

However, I really want to discuss the incredible worldbuilding and the attention to detail that went into the post-apocalyptic world and how everything works. It's as though our MC An Zhe and we, the readers, are going through this together.

I don't want to say too much about the characters because I didn't find it interesting or have a connection to any of them. An Zhe is incredibly sweet and pure, while Lu Feng is a highly interesting character. Even though I'm not a huge science fiction lover, I feel like this one has truly given me a whole new perspective.

Looking forward to the next volume.
