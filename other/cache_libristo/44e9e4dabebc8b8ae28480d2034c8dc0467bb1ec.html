Root Rot

Saskia Nislow

3.44
6,491 ratings1,581 reviews
Rate this book
Come meet The Liar, The Secret Keeper, The Crybaby, and the rest.
Nine children travel with their families to their Grandfather's vacation property, where strange things begin to happen: eyes blink from the bottom up, mushrooms ooze blood, people's faces don't hang right anymore-except they do, once you look more closely. Transformations warp the children's sense of time and place, the very land itself seeming to encroach upon them.
As The Liar watches the children succumb one by one to an unknown fate, she must make sense of absent stars in the night sky, vials of amber liquid that taste of milk, a funny little rope tied in knots. She's faced with a choice: join or resist, only the choice is not so simple.
Set in The Grandfather's Lake House as he continues to extend his property lines and told in the eerie we of the children, Root Rot explores predatory family dynamics, the boundaries of bodies and home, and how individuals choose to participate in or push back against structures that would harm them.

FUNGAL. 🍄 FOLKLORIC. 🕯️ WTF IS HAPPENING. 🫯 A strange, damp horror novella about toxic family systems, childhood perception, and the rot that takes hold when harm becomes so embedded in a family that no one knows how to name it anymore.

This book left me unsettled, sad, impressed, and still a little unsure what to do with it.

The atmosphere is 10/10. It has this humid, overgrown, summer-at-the-lake quality where everything should feel nostalgic and familiar, but the woods are wrong, the house is wrong, the adults are wrong, and the children are trying to understand a world that keeps shifting beneath them. The folk horror and fungal horror are so effective here. Mushrooms, bodies, inheritance, family roles, land, secrecy, and sacrifice all start to blur together until the book feels soft, living, and suffocating all at once.

The narration is one of the eeriest parts of the book. The children are not introduced through traditional names, but through the roles they occupy within the family: the liar, the crybaby, the secret keeper, the one who runs away, etc. That collective first-person plural narration took me a little while to settle into, and I did struggle at first with the rhythm of it and with keeping everyone oriented in my head. Once I adjusted to it, though, the choice became deeply effective. It makes the children feel connected and erased at the same time, already folded into the family story before they ever get the chance to understand themselves outside of it.

I also struggled a little with the repetition that comes from the structure itself. Because the story moves through a collective child perspective, with different children experiencing variations of the same wrongness, some of the body horror and uncanny imagery started to echo itself for me. There are only so many times I can feel the impact of something being almost human, almost familiar, almost itself, before the language begins to lose some of its sharpness. I understand why that repetition exists—it adds to the looping, disorienting quality of the book—but it did make certain sections feel less precise for me.

The horror here works through dread, wrongness, and the specific helplessness of being a child surrounded by adults who know more than they are saying. The supernatural elements are disturbing, but the family dynamics sharpen the horror further: the silence, the roles, the avoidance, the inherited harm, and the way everyone keeps participating in something rotten because it has become part of the environment.

The plot is tragic and sad, with a mythic quality that makes the whole thing feel older than the family itself (yesss, folk horror! 😌🤌🏽) I did want more clarity in places, and I can see the structure either completely working for readers or becoming something they have to push through. But I appreciated how strange it was. In my eyes, this is a story about family as ecosystem, family as inheritance, family as infection, and the terrifying realization that what is growing beneath everything may have been there all along.

A very strange book, but an enjoyable one. The fungal horror is beautifully gross, the atmosphere is thick and eerie, and the whole thing left me feeling like I had wandered out of the woods with something still clinging to me.

3.5 stars from me!

This will work best for readers who like strange, atmospheric horror with folk and fungal elements, toxic family systems, child narrators, and more dread than explanation. Avoid it if you need a clear plot, clean answers, or a cast that is easy to keep separate from the start. Root Rot is slippery by design, and that will either be the appeal or the frustration. 🍄

“He hadn’t really understood before, he mused vaguely, how small he was in comparison to his body. There was so much space inside of him. So much empty space. Filling up.”

Growing up on the Dutch-German border, I remember exploring my family's bookshelves filled with decades old books they held onto. One of them terrified me in particular. A book of classical regional fairytales. Not the Disney-variety, but the gruesome originals, filled with folk-horror and bizarre imagery. Many of them, including some of the lesser-known niche ones, had me looking over my shoulder whenever I was out in nature.
Root Rot felt like it came from the pages of this book. Bizarre, unsettling, but strangely hypnotic; I was under its spell.

Our story begins with nine children arriving at the grandfather's vacation property, for a family-gathering following the death of grandma. We don’t learn any of their names, as they’re only referred to by a moniker (“the Liar”, “the Secret Keeper”, the “Oldest” etc.), nor do we get any backstory on them. Whilst the adults convene inside the house, the kids explore the forest and gardens around the Lake House, where they encounter increasingly strange and unsettling events.
All of this is told through the voice of an unnamed narrator, watching from just outside the frame, referring to the kids as “we/us”. Are they one of the 9 children, a 10th child, or something else entirely?

Nislow proves herself a master of atmosphere. From page 1, she nails the “uncanny-valley” feeling, and truly shines in her ability to defamiliarize the familiar. Visuals and events that might otherwise be normal or even nostalgic are warped, transformed and twisted into unrecognizable shapes. It’s a brand of horror that I’m a sucker for, and Nislow executes it to perfection here.
Similarly, I loved how the story “flirts” with familiar figures of European mythology, whilst completely making it her own as well. There are hints at , but the eventual answer to the mysteries are all Nislow’s own twist of mythology.
Root Rot requires quite some suspension of disbelieve, and a bit of work from the reader. It intentionally keeps you at a distance and in the dark for quite a while, and while it adds to the fever-dream-feeling, I can understand how not every reader will appreciate that. In its current dose, this being a novella and all, it was perfect for me.
That being said, if you your fairytale/folk-horror, nature-horror or even sporror with a hint of the bizarre and uncanny: this is one that’s 100% worth your time.

Many thanks to Creature Publishing for providing me with an ARC in exchange for an honest review. All opinions are my own.

Sub-Genre/Themes: Modern gothic, LBGTQIA+, vacation, sporror, family drama, body horror

Writing Style: compelling, unique, open to interpretation, experimental, rotating or collective POV "we"

What You Need to Know: "Nine children travel with their families to their Grandfather's vacation property, where strange things begin to happen."

My Reading Experience: Reading Root Rot feels like sitting around at a family gathering, listening to your cousins retell that one wild thing that happened years ago, only now it’s embellished or has an extra layer of mythology.
Told from the collective perspective of the children, Root Rot plunges you into the strange, secretive world of kids left to their own devices while the adults do whatever it is that adults do.
The children don’t have names in the traditional sense, there are “The Liar,” “The Baby,” and “The One with the Beautiful Voice", labels that feel like something assigned to the child during a situation and it stuck. You’re branded as “the dramatic one”, “the crybaby” or “the troublemaker,” and breaking free from that identity is nearly impossible.
And then there’s the way Nislow plays with reality. Are the kids just making things up, or is something truly rotten beneath the surface? The story spirals from something familiar into darker territory. Some scenes, like when The Baby goes missing, will be burned in my mind forever. I loved how Nislow was able to give glimpses into our collective nostalgia for family vacations at a vacation house while twisting it into something alien and gross.

Final Recommendation: Perfect for readers who aren't afraid of the strange and unusual. Southern gothic vibes, sporror (spore horror) and fungus, gross vegetation and eco-horrors, toxic family dynamic, fever-dream storytelling. It's fun to develop theories about subtext and symbolism. I was a little let down by the conclusion--I'm not sure what I was expecting, but I did feel a little ho-hum after I finished. Loved the journey though, a lot.

this hazy horror novella is an odd fever dream, told in the collective first person plural POV of a group of siblings and cousins staying at the family's lake house.

the first half feels unbelievably similar to another eerie novella, idle grounds. both have the plural "we" POV, and both have a dreamy, vague narration that puts the reader in a childlike state of mind. both books also have nostalgic cousinly dynamics, and the feeling of being part of a group of kids, wholly separate from any adults. i cannot believe these two books were released just a month apart and neither was influenced by the other.

there's a detached sense of fear - the terror of the uncanny, of being powerless of a child, of knowing in your gut that something isn't right. the horror elements are also wonderfully woodsy, with a fun mycological element. there's a theme of inevitability that i think pairs well with the childhood setting. when you're a kid, lacking agency, you're at the mercy of whatever is going to happen. very moody doomy vibes!

the characters (the liar, the secret keeper, the crybaby, the baby, the girl twin, the boy twin, the one with the beautiful voice, the one who runs away, and the oldest) are hard to keep straight at first. it wasn't until the book was halfway over that i finally had a feel for who was who, but maybe that's intentional. they are almost archetypes of young childhood, defined by how they are perceived by others.

i loved the trans rep - quiet and implied, but so crucial to the liar's character, and so dismal and demoralizing. the other children accept the liar as a girl, but there are indications that the adults do not. this division adds to the feeling of powerlessness that permeates the book.

unfortunately, this really lost me at the trippy, confusing, ambiguous end. but there are some really jarring lynchian moments that i loved, and that energy makes me want to lean into the strange. i will definitely be interested in any forthcoming books from nislow!
