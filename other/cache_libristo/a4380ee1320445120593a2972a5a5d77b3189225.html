I think the easiest way to get started is the cover. We've all been told not to judge a book by its cover: advice that we gleefully ignore every chance we get. I took one look at the Butcher of Nazareth's cover and thought "Oh, this is going to be metal as fuck." Reading the summary only amplified that impression.

To be clear, BoN is, indeed, metal as fuck.

It is also slow-burn, modern apocrypha about grief and our desperation to correct mistakes that are impossible to take back. Our protagonist is a heartbreaking figure. He's also (at various points) warm, tender, and funny. He's a dad, and at times he's the dad that a lot of us would like to be. And at other times, he's a nightmare: a broken patriarch with so much blood on his hands it could make you sick.

Hay's handle on characters is what gives this book its real wings. Everyone here feels like meat and bone: real and tangible. This extends from our protagonist to his quarry, who, in the end, is just another man filled with fear, self-doubt, and the everyday flaws and quirks that we all have.

At various points through my reading of this book, I cried, I laughed, I gagged, and I image searched the word "sakin".

And every now and then, I did indeed mutter "Yeah -- that's metal as *fuck*."

A haunting and bleak meditation on duty, revenge, fervor, and devotion, this novel grips you by the throat from the first page and doesn’t let go. The style is sparse, giving a sense of open landscapes filled with regrets and broken promises. You can feel the grit of the desert sand, hear the shouting of the crowd and smell the wild stink of untamed beasts, an entirely visceral reading experience, but accomplished without thick exposition or purple prose. Following the Butcher on his unasked-for quest is an experience that lights all the senses aflame, and the harsh jagged edges to the writing capture the sentiment well.

That said, this novel isn’t unforgiving. It isn’t crass or blasphemous or cynical and mean just for the sake of it. Yes, there are eruptions of violence, and yes the subject matter may be challenging for certain faith traditions, but it is all in the service of story, not spectacle. And this is a story of pain, of desolation and loss. Religious frenzy, yes, feverish devotion in the hope for absolution. But also loss, shame, guilt, and fear. This is a story of knowing you don’t deserve redemption but hoping your deeds can still be of service. A story of accepting that sometimes it takes a monster to stop the monstrous, and conviction that is a skin you can wear, you already wear, you have stitched to you from the inside out. The reader is invited to empathize with our main character, with the Butcher, not because we delight in bloodshed but because we abhor it. He goes nameless for most of the novel, and in many ways he is a cypher for the reader, for our fears about what we can do in the face of an impossible task.

The story moves at a steady pace, an outer journey that runs parallel to an inner one. But the inner journey is not about finding oneself, about developing self-confidence. It is a journey of self-destruction, of giving oneself over to your faith and your wish that you can sacrifice yourself to make the world a better place. Our Butcher faces many subtle but clever tribulations, forcing the reader to contemplate who is the goat and who is the lamb, and what kind of person is tasked with religious visions and tribulations. There is a softness to him, one that is hidden by sharp-edged blades and jagged horns, by thorns and stingers and everything that cuts and maims, inside and out. We cannot embrace his monstrosity without seeing his humanity, his softness, his longing for redemption. Regardless of your faith tradition or lack thereof this story forces you to question ideas of destiny, of passion, and of responsibility. It refuses easy answers or categorizations, and disallows complacency in our assurances that the stories we have woven about ourselves are truth, or Truth, instead reminding us that they are but a balm, personal mythologies that soothe our hearts and justify our missteps. There are no heroes, only broken people wrestling with their mistakes as they try to live up to expectations foisted on them, either by society or by the Divine, recognizing the difference between those may be more slight than we think.

If I am going to be very honest, I did not think I would enjoy The Butcher of Nazareth considering that I know fuck all about the Bible story (oops), but holy hell, did David Scott Hay prove me wrong. This literary historical horror is an absolute fever dream of a book, a hallucinatory nightmare that gets progressively weirder and more horrifying with every turn of the page, and I love how its emotional weight honestly cuts deeper than a butcher's knife.

Now, the premise of The Butcher of Nazareth is fairly simple, as we follow a man plagued by visions of what Christianity will become, and he decides Jesus must die before the religion's future atrocities can happen. Except nothing about this book is simple or particularly reasonable, and I was completely fascinated by how far Hay was willing to push any and all boundaries with this New Testament prequel story. He just keeps escalating the violence and madness, throwing increasingly questionable situations at you while somehow never losing sight of the humanity at the core of it all, and I loved spiralling together with Titus.

I mean, this man is absolutely one of the most uncomfortable yet deeply compelling protagonist perspectives I have read in a while, and I really appreciated that he never felt like a symbolic shell of a figure simply inserted into Jesus's story to serve a narrative purpose. He is a complex mess of a human, with all the ugly contradictions that come with being one, and I was unexpectedly moved by the exploration of fatherhood, child loss, morality, losing faith and finding it again, and the cost of rage and isolation through his eyes. It is just so heartbreaking to follow a man who is so consumed by grief that he convinces himself his suffering has given him a purpose, and the way that his narration became more and more unreliable as his sanity started to fracture had me in an absolute chokehold.

And look, I am sure I could have enjoyed The Butcher of Nazareth even more if I had been more familiar with the Bible, but I actually think it is a testament to Hay's storytelling skills that this works so well without that prior knowledge. Seeing this unexpected interpretation of Jesus as a heavy-set, honey-loving man who mostly just wants to provide for his family was such a wild surprise, and I loved how that gave the story this strange intimacy that makes everything Titus does feel even more horrifying. The contrast between this very human, almost disarmingly ordinary portrayal and the increasingly deranged circumstances surrounding him truly works wonderfully, and the level of anxiety I felt for these people's fates when the Butcher finally loses all sense of sanity and launches his attack on Nazareth was absolutely dreadful in the best way.

Hay's prose is also deeply immersive and emotive despite, or perhaps because of, how sparse it is, and I think his background in playwriting and screenwriting really shines through in the way he builds his scenes. The visuals and scenery are hellishly stark and vivid, and the atmosphere is so wonderfully suffocating that I felt like I was walking right there along Titus and his emotional support goat. I also cannot recommend the audiobook highly enough, because Brian Alan Hill captures the bleak atmosphere and escalating madness so well with his reserved yet emotive performance, and it only made the whole experience more immersive, for better or worse.

And look, I am still not entirely sure what the hell happened in the final part of The Butcher of Nazareth, despite listening to it all twice over, but I honestly think that ambiguity is part of the brilliance. I do not necessarily need every piece of this particular nightmare neatly explained to me, especially when the uncertainty fits so perfectly with Titus’s fractured perspective and the story’s increasingly blurred line between faith, madness, prophecy and reality. The emotional beats still hit, Hay absolutely nailed the epilogue, and I love that I can keep ruminating on this book for a long time to come.

I probably respect The Butcher of Nazareth more than I fully enjoyed it, but I also don't think this is really the kind of story you come to for enjoyment. It might be short, but it packs one hell of a big punch, and I love how much Hay manages to accomplish in such a relatively small space. It is weird, uncomfortable, brutal, viciously violent, mordibly funny, and ultimately so much more emotionally devastating than I expected, so I would absolutely recommend it if you are in the mood for a devilishly clever biblical horror tale with a deeply tragic human heart.

A brick through the stained glass window.

BWAF SINISTER SELECTION
BWAF Score: 7/10

TL;DR: David Scott Hay’s The Butcher of Nazareth is a blasphemous fever-vision: biblical horror as contamination event, prophecy as infection, and faith as a weaponized plague. It’s nasty-poetic, hallucinatory, and proudly indie in its refusal to behave. Darkly funny, brutally committed, and impossible to mistake for safe “elevated” horror.

The Butcher of Nazareth by David Scott Hay is an anti-hagiographic biblical nightmare that treats the Christ story like an apocalyptic contamination event. It is ugly, deliberate, and weird with intent.

This book escalates like a curse tightening its grip, and it does not give a shit if you’re comfortable. It opens with a mission statement like a cracked commandment: Titus the Butcher believes his task is “simple though arduous,” to kill one more child, except the “child” is a grown man now. From the jump, the prose is nasty-poetic and tactile, full of steaming filth, glistening innards, splintering wood, and the casual brutality of a man whose hands know how to make bodies stop. It’s also darkly funny in that scorched-earth way where humor is just the nervous system trying to stay upright while the world rots around you. Titus is not a noble assassin. He’s a grieving father, a former participant in Herod’s slaughter, a tradesman whose “calling” has turned him into an instrument. He walks through Judea with an unnaturally large one-horned goat like it’s his demonic emotional support animal, and every step drags a future behind it.

Titus, once conscripted into “the Culling” of Bethlehem’s infants, is plagued by visions of what Christianity will become, and he decides Jesus must die before the religion’s future atrocities can happen. The novel follows Titus across Bethlehem, Jerusalem, and Nazareth as he hunts the pre-ministry Nazarene, accumulating bodies, confessions, and ritualized wrongness, while the line between divine instruction and delusion keeps getting thinner. The dread isn’t “is God real.” The dread is “what if faith is the vector and history is the disease.”

Visions function like infection. Titus gets these spikes of revelation, flashes of future crusades and inquisitions and flame-deluge apocalypse, and they drive him like a nail through the skull. The book doesn’t treat prophecy as mystical comfort, but as horror surveillance. Titus sees the “end” as a world burned to ash and glass, and those images are not pretty wallpaper. They’re operational justification. That constant pressure, that sense of being commanded by something that might be holy or might be madness with good branding, is what keeps the book humming. You don’t get to relax. You don’t get to step outside the fever. You are in it.

That escalation is structural, too. Book I (Bethlehem) builds the engine and immediately starts poisoning it. Titus arrives hauling a wounded youth, bargains his way into a midwife’s home, and gets pulled into a grotesque remix of nativity myth as commerce and performance. The midwife, Cassia, is one of the novel’s best inventions because she isn’t a saint or a strawman. She’s sharp, exhausted, practical, and spiritually furious. She teaches Titus sutures. She makes him butcher lamb. She talks about grief like it’s a tool. Then the book drops one of its first true “oh fuck” turns: Cassia reveals she keeps her embalmed dead infant preserved in a chest, hoping the divine king will resurrect him someday. That’s the moment the book stops being “revisionist Bible thriller” and becomes something closer to blasphemous folk-Gothic ritual object. Titus is forced to hold that tiny corpse. He is forced to ask whether he killed it. He is forced to watch maternal faith curdle into a demand. It’s intimate and obscene and absolutely not playing for tasteful prestige vibes.

This is close third that stays glued to Titus’s interior logic, with feverish interludes that read like scripture having a panic attack. Reliability is the whole game. Titus is convinced he is chosen. He is also clearly capable of monstrous rationalization. The narration has a hypnotic chant quality, returning to refrains like “The Lord will guide my hand,” until the phrase becomes both prayer and indictment. The tonal consistency is impressive. Even when the book gets grotesquely funny or theatrically biblical, it keeps the same scorched devotional pitch. It’s a sustained incantation, and that’s why it works.

The book refuses to make Titus purely symbolic. He’s ashamed, proud, tender in flashes, and then immediately capable of making someone a carcass. He forms relationships that feel like pressure systems: Cassia as mirror, the Roman legionary as unwanted intimacy and looming consequence, the Pharisee as the most terrifying kind of kinship. Later, when Titus brushes up against the Nazarene’s family, the domestic textures matter. Bread, honey, small talk, quiet resentments. That mundane friction makes the dread sharper, because it turns myth into something you can smell. It also underscores the book’s meanest idea: history isn’t made by marble statues. It’s made by people having arguments in kitchens while monsters in power take notes.

The imagery is concrete and memorable: splintering wood, mangers as props and as evidence, bodies opened and stitched, honeycomb and wax and stings, goat horn regrowth like an obscene miracle. The goat is an inspired choice because it keeps the book hovering between allegory and creature-feature menace. Sometimes it’s comic. Sometimes it’s a witness. Sometimes it feels like the book’s true demon, the thing that keeps pushing Titus into the next station of suffering. When the apiary and bees become part of the choreography, the prose tips into hallucinatory physicality, the kind that makes you itch while you’re reading.

Hay knows what to show. The violence is not coy, and it’s rarely “cool.” It’s messy, humiliating, and often framed through labor: carving, cutting, stitching, dragging, cleaning. There’s a sequence of communal punishment that plays like social infection, not justice theater, and it’s one of the moments where the book’s thesis stops being abstract and starts being bodily. Bodies do not disappear cleanly. Grief becomes an object you carry. Shame becomes a tool people use on each other. And when the book turns ritualistic, it does it with viciousness.

David Scott Hay comes out of a background that includes playwriting and screenwriting, and you can feel that stagecraft in how scenes escalate and how dialogue can turn from banter to threat without a warm-up. He’s also written satire and genre hybrids, which explains why The Butcher of Nazareth has both contempt for institutions and a willingness to go full sacrilege without blinking. This isn’t a writer discovering religion as an aesthetic. This is a writer using religious narrative as a weaponized container for horror, and doing it in a way that feels proudly indie: too bodily, too specific, too willing to offend to be sanded down into “elevated but safe.”

The book is formally and conceptually unhinged in a deliberate, indie-only way. It’s not “what if Jesus, but edgy.” It’s “what if the Christ story is the origin of a future plague, and the plague is faith plus power plus permission.” The book’s big swing is that it treats salvation as a dangerous idea, a Get Out of Hell Free abstraction that can be used to launder atrocities. It makes Titus a kind of anti-Judas, a man trying to murder the mechanism of grace before it metastasizes. That’s a daring thesis, and the book actually commits to it instead of winking.

The power comes from monolithic intensity. Sometimes the thesis-forward drive flattens certain character beats into sermon-ish momentum, and the relentless pitch can feel less like a perfectly calibrated narrative machine and more like a sustained jeremiad. I didn’t mind that. It’s part of the posture. This book wants to prophesize. It wants to accuse. It wants to crawl into your moral imagination and piss on the rug. But if you’re a reader who needs modulation and breathable pacing, you might find the incantation exhausting.

If you want a clean, filmable thriller with tasteful theological ambiguity, this will feel like a brick through a stained-glass window. But if you want indie horror that’s actually willing to be blasphemous, bodily, and prophetic, and you can tolerate (or enjoy) a book that reads like an extended, feverish denunciation, this is where it’s at. Read this motherfucker, now!

Read if you want biblical horror that treats the gospel like a biohazard and doesn’t apologize.

Skip if you want a neat, filmable thriller rather than a monolithic, hallucinatory sermon from hell.

I can be a bit like Charlie Brown with the football when it comes to fictional reimaginings of the New Testament. No matter how many times I'm disappointed, it's a conceptual template I continue to seek out, hoping against hope that the next one will be the one that connects. As such, when David Scott Hay first sent me The Butcher of Nazareth, I was both excited (as a fan of both the form, and his previous work), and a little worried (as a reader already let down by the more famous likes of Philip Pullman and Christopher Moore). But fear not, brothers and sisters - followers of the good books - for I bring you glad tidings of great joy. This one is rad as hell.

Now full disclosure, I ended up writing the introduction for this book, so I'm necessarily biased, and much of what I have to say about it went into that work (which I hope you'll all read). But still, speaking strictly as a reader, The Butcher of Nazareth is a wicked good time. Revolving around Titus, a man conscripted into Herod's mass infanticide (here dubbed "the culling") and subsequently plagued by visions of the future apocalypse he believes will result from that project's failure to eliminate the Christ child, this is a dark, deft, and at times sinfully clever traipse across the shifting desert sands of myth and history in which he sets out to finish the job (with a bigass goat in tow).

In the hands of a lesser author, this whole idea might well have resulted in a one-note polemic against the church, or worse yet, a string of mindless, naughty boy sacrileges, but Hay takes great pains to imbue his characters, both real and imagined, with nuance and depth. His depiction of the young Jesus as a potbellied hippie frolicking with his beloved Mary Magdalene feels sweet and sexy in ways even Kazantzakis wouldn't dare, and in contrasting the small, lunatic horrors of Titus's damaged psyche with the rampant, global horrors still being wrought in the name of a corrupt, crusading religious movement, he offers us a vessel for all our lost and broken faiths.

These characters sense their own importance - their place at the dawn of something bigger than themselves, and The Butcher of Nazareth uses them to ask big questions - namely, would the world be better off if someone had just smothered baby Jesus in his manger? - without insisting on solid answers (as all good Biblical speculation should). And while I've still got a few more Charlie Brown kicks at the New Testament football in my reading plans for this year (Robert Graves, I'm lookin' at you), I feel confident in saying that no one will ever approach the greatest story ever told quite like David Scott Hay. At the risk of a little naughty boy sacrilege myself, I'd say he fuckin' nailed it.

The Butcher of Nazareth is one of the more captivating and unique books I've read in some time. Years after King Herod ordered a culling of male children under 2 in response to the possibility of a competing king being born, one of the men involved is called by god to track down the one child who escaped the culling - Jesus of Nazareth.

I was so impressed with the writing and the storytelling here. We follow The Butcher as he travels across Bethlehem, Jerusalem, and Nazareth searching for his target, all the while dealing with the ghosts of his past life. The structure is really interesting, the whole book being three large chapters with a prologue and an epilogue., and written in a very biblical way. Even though we don't have short chapters, something about the writing had me wanting to keep going. We get great emotional depth to our characters with an incredibly unique story, and stunning imagery. As the intro suggests, Hay dares to tell a story and ask questions about a time when we get little information, filling in a unique alternate history to the world of Jesus before his adulthood.

This book also deals with some really heavy topics - child loss, grief, faith, losing faith and finding it again, and the cost of rage and isolation. It is visceral and violent, and filled with iconic imagery (we get a goat, bees, ritualistic rites and so much more), as well as a Jesus who is not at all what we are familiar with. And in all that there is also beauty, and the idea that we need people to help us through life, and that carrying rage and hate bears a significant cost.

Full disclosure, I think folks who are very strictly religious will struggle with this one, and there are some trigger warnings you should check before reading, especially surrounding children and violence. However, if you like religious horror, if you are open to exploring a well known person and time of history in a very unique way, and if you love a book that tells a good horror story while also dealing with heavy themes in an incredibly unique and artistic way, you should really give this one a try!

**"Modern, evangelical Cormac McCarthy."**

I have read McCarthy's most celebrated works, and after merely 40–50 pages, I was usually locked in. McCarthy had a way with words, a prose style that, to me, remains remarkably unique. The man possessed the extraordinary talent of pouring philosophy, existentialism, and thought-provoking ideas directly into your mind whilst telling stories about horrific worlds that seemed all too familiar, yet alien enough to make you question the very nature of human existence.

However...

I am beginning to wonder whether that Kirkus quote is merely a marketing buzzword, designed to lure unsuspecting readers like flies towards the ointment.

Because this novel does not read like McCarthy. It is nowhere near the prose of McCarthy.

Hay's sentences are short, simple, repetitive, and sometimes seemingly stitched together without much rhythm or purpose. There is little philosophical abstraction, save for the occasional archaic or apocryphal expression, interspersed with historical customs and references to weapons.

Now, I am not saying Hay is a bad writer. The jury is still out on that. I am, however, still waiting for this supposedly horrific, terrifying novel to actually become horrifying.

I will say that the stoning scene was particularly well written. It was evocative, and Hay's short, staccato sentences seemed to imitate the very stones being cast. Each sentence struck, stopped, and made way for the next. Here, his minimalist approach served the violence rather than merely describing it.

But unlike McCarthy, whose prose could transform an act of violence into something almost metaphysical, Hay's sentences tend to remain short, simple, descriptive, and confined to the immediate moment. They do not drag you beyond the violence and into an uncaring macrocosm where human suffering becomes almost insignificant against the sheer indifference of existence.

At least, not yet.

Maybe Hay is saving the best for last.

And this brings me to what is perhaps my greatest grievance with this novel so far, although, ironically, it is not entirely a grievance with the novel itself.

**My quarrel is with Kirkus, not necessarily with Scott Hay.**

I would probably never have bought this book had it not been for that comparison with Cormac McCarthy. That single sentence was not some insignificant embellishment on the cover. It was the very reason I became interested in the novel in the first place.

When you invoke a writer of McCarthy's calibre to promote another author's work, you are doing more than merely suggesting a vague literary resemblance. You are creating expectations, particularly among readers who are intimately familiar with the author's work.

You are promising something. And when that promise remains unfulfilled, the resulting disappointment should not automatically be laid at the feet of the writer whose book you have just sold. This is where I believe Kirkus does a disservice to both authors. Hay is not Cormac McCarthy, nor should he have to be. He has his own voice, his own stylistic preferences, and presumably his own literary ambitions. Yet here I am, measuring his work against that of another author because somebody decided that attaching McCarthy's name to the cover would be an effective way of selling the novel. And it worked. I bought it.

But in doing so, that comparison has also contaminated my reading experience. Instead of discovering Scott Hay on his own terms, I entered his novel expecting a particular kind of prose, atmosphere, and philosophical depth. That is unfair to Hay, and I recognise that. Yet I refuse to pretend that the comparison is irrelevant simply because it has proven inconvenient. If a publisher or reviewer invokes McCarthy to sell me a novel, then I believe I am perfectly entitled to examine that claim critically. If I really pushed myself, I could perhaps describe Hay as McCarthy-like, but only because his novel contains moments of extreme violence, because many of his characters remain unnamed, and because the Butcher himself occasionally resembles a sort of Judge Holden light. But these are superficial similarities. Violence alone does not make a writer McCarthy-esque. Neither does withholding a character's name or creating an enigmatic, murderous antagonist. What made McCarthy extraordinary was not simply the horror he depicted, but the language through which that horror acquired its philosophical and existential weight.

To reduce his literary identity to violence, nameless characters, and disturbing imagery is to misunderstand much of what made his work so remarkable. And to burden Hay with that comparison risks obscuring whatever he might have achieved on his own terms.
I have therefore made a conscious effort to abandon the expectations Kirkus planted in my head. Unfortunately, even without the McCarthy comparison, I still find Hay's prose rather uneven. His writing is sometimes effective, sometimes repetitive, and occasionally quite bland. The dinner scene in the Midwife chapter, for instance, felt particularly uninspired.

It is not the Butcher's past that interests me particularly, nor am I terribly invested in his mission. I also have a fairly good idea of what that goat is doing in the story.
But I do love the Butcher's mannerisms. There is something deeply unsettling about the way he conducts himself, something that suggests a considerably more disturbing character than Hay has so far allowed us to explore. And perhaps that is where my frustration truly lies.
I wish Hay would carve deeper into this man's psyche, or at least lend his inscrutability a greater sense of weight and menace. I want to be buried beneath labyrinthine sentences that drag me through his child-slaughtering nightmares, until I can almost smell the blood and feel the suffocating atmosphere of the world he inhabits.
I do not necessarily need the Butcher to be explained to me. In fact, explaining him too thoroughly might diminish the very horror he represents.

What little I have read of Chapter Two has already renewed my interest, so I am not prepared to condemn Hay or his novel just yet.

I will update this review as I peel through the remaining pages.

Who knows what horrors lurk at the end of the tunnel?

Since optimism is not a pigment suitable to my palette I will also add that if this book is found lacking, I will not necessarily put the blame on the author, it might simply be the false bill of goods sold to me by Kirkus. I was told this was McCarthy-esque and yet it doesn't rear it's head in the prose so far... I will avoid the publisher if this was novel was sold to what I call a false promise.
