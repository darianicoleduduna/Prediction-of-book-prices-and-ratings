The End of Everything

M. John Harrison

2.93

Phillip Tennent makes his living at the tideline, collecting artefacts that wash up from the Channel. It's been years since the crisis changed everything, but its exact nature remains obscured. Government barely functions, the seas are full of new creatures, Europe has been mislaid. It feels like the end.

Now Phillip has fished out of the water an object he can't keep. A creature that keeps changing. An artefact he must take inland, before it destroys everything he thinks he knows.

Now Winner of the Booker Prize 2026
The nomination of Jarvis Cocker as a Booker judge made me hope for a list that embraces the weird and the whacky, and I was not disappointed: Not only is "The End of Everything" genre lit (SciFi), which makes some self-declared high-end literati shudder, it also tells an intentionally opaque story about an alien invasion - at least on the obvious plot level. The whole vibe remindede me of Brautigan's classic In Watermelon Sugar, and I'm sure the iGhetti, Harrison's alien species, are not only named like this as a nod to Apple, thus sticking to the theme of a super intelligence that is taking over an increasingly disoriented humanity, but also points to Brautigan's concept of iDeath - "In Watermelon Sugar" revolves around this equally enigmatic concept, also prompting readers to fill in the blanks regarding what is even going on.

Harrison gives us a dystopia set in Britain in the near future, after "the crisis" - we don't know what exactly happened, but the iGhetti, spectral entities, have taken London, the government is merely functioning, mainland Europe has become even more detached, and there is no reliable source of information anymore, neither in form of legacy nor social media, so people don't know what is happening regarding the war against the iGhetti: This is an eternal now of insecurity, a dangerous world of instability. Our protagonist Phillip Tennent is a beachcomber who lives with his artistic aunt Marnie, and one day he drags an object out of the sea that is somehow connected to the iGhetti, an artefact that keeps changing and evolving, becoming more and more dangerous...

There is no one way to read this novel, but I was constantly thinking of AI and Silicon Valley dreams of transhumanism and super-intelligence, of an ever-evolving technology becoming maybe not conscious, but alive and efficiently following a script by learning and adapting. And then there's the people: They cut ties with other countries (there's reverberations of Brexit here) to increase the power of the financial haven by getting rid of regulations, then this haven (London) is taken by aliens (aka the technology developed by the richest of the rich) enforcing their own agenda, and then the periphery is tranformed. And I'm so curious to read all the other interpretations this story invites.

This is truly something else for a Booker list, and it's a novel that's particularly suited for discussion - and that's the power of the Booker, to invite global discussions about books.

Update: I'm in shock. It won the Booker. Congratulations.

Now shortlisted for the 2026 Booker Prize - Justice was done. I can understand that people can't connect with it, but there was something not right about it being the worst-rated book on the longlist.

Watch out for this one. It smells like a winner.

Tuesday, when the Booker longlist was announced, I was surprised by many books. It happens every year. The End of Everything wasn't one of them. I've seen this book appear on my radar constantly in the past months and, of course, I ignored it because I rarely read sci-fi. The Booker made me realise the obvious: word of mouth came from spaces that weren't necessarily genre-friendly. So don't make this mistake when you see this (maybe for the first time) on a list that this year seems to have more weird titles than usual. This will be the one that deserves the inclusion, and maybe even more. Perhaps the time has come for M. John Harrison to be more than a cult author. We will see.

This post-apocalyptic novel, about a world where an unknown entity has changed the course of humanity in ways that aren't really clear, is the kind of blank space that offers the reader the opportunity to tell what they see in it, and maybe what their fears are. iGhetti has invaded London, but we don't know what it is or what it wants. It could be the effect of the climate crisis, aliens, or just AI.

Probably the most fascinating part is what humans do when they encounter the unknown. The answer may surprise you: they survive. Adaptation is just the fastest way back to the ordinary.

Phillip Tennet, a beachcomber who just discovered an artefact that may offer the answers everyone is searching for, and his aunt Marnie, an artist who shows signs of dementia, aren't really anything more than two ordinary people. Even their violent outbursts aren't that special for a dystopian environment. Maybe the (non)response to those acts shows the unpredictability of living in an unruled place, but their life is pretty regular. The end of the world can be a continuous event, just like a war, and when you are in the middle of it, you learn to see your life as best as you can. Marnie lives like any other person battling old age and dementia, trapped between lost memories and false nostalgic ones, while Phillip, like any other man close to middle age, can still hope for another future, maybe in another place that maybe was untouched by this entity.

Because, like a character says, the end means that a new beginning is around the corner. The question is who will be at the top. Can humans willingly surrender control to something else, or learn to coexist?

This is a profound novel that can be read in so many ways, and I think its value comes from offering all those possibilities and making readers see, in this story, something that happens right now in the world. And here is another idea: the end of everything doesn't matter. The end is personal, and it will always remain that way.

I honestly went into this one wanting to love it, because the premise sounded so strange, eerie, and full of possibilities. A world changed by some mysterious crisis, seas filled with unfamiliar creatures, a government barely functioning, and parts of Europe seemingly lost? Sign me up! I was expecting something dark, unsettling, and completely different from anything I had read before. Unfortunately, this ended up being a pretty confusing reading experience for me, and I struggled to connect with the story more than I wanted to.

Half the time, I genuinely had no idea what was going on. I would be reading along, thinking I was finally starting to understand where the story was heading, only for things to take another turn and leave me feeling lost all over again. The plot felt all over the place, and I constantly found myself trying to piece together what was happening, what certain things meant, and how everything was supposed to fit together. Instead of feeling intrigued by the mystery, I often felt confused and frustrated.

The idea of Phillip Tennent collecting strange artefacts washed up along the Channel was one of the things that initially caught my attention. There is something incredibly creepy about a world that has been fundamentally changed, where the ocean holds things, nobody fully understands and the people living in this broken reality are still trying to figure out what happened. The mysterious, ever-changing creature he discovers and the journey to take it inland sounded like the perfect setup for a weird, atmospheric story. I really wanted to get swept up in that mystery and feel the tension of discovering something that could change everything.

But for me, the execution just didn't come together the way I had hoped. The world itself was fascinating in concept, but I struggled to understand it well enough to feel fully immersed in it. So much seemed deliberately unclear, and while I can appreciate a story that leaves room for interpretation, there is a difference between being intrigued by unanswered questions and feeling like I am missing pieces of the story altogether. Unfortunately, I spent far too much time in the second category.

I also struggled with the overall flow. The story felt disjointed at times, and I had trouble finding something solid to hold on to as I moved through it. I wanted more clarity, more direction, and more moments that helped everything click into place. I kept waiting for that moment when all the strange pieces would finally connect and I would understand what the story had been building toward, but I never felt as satisfied or grounded as I wanted to be.

I can see how readers who enjoy unusual, abstract, and challenging science fiction might appreciate what this book is trying to do. There is an unsettling atmosphere here, and the idea of a world slowly becoming unrecognizable is genuinely interesting. The sense that humanity is living through the aftermath of something enormous, without fully understanding what has happened or what might come next, has plenty of potential. I just wish I had connected with the storytelling more because the concept really did have me curious.

Overall, this was a 2.5-star read for me. I didn't hate everything about it, and I can appreciate the ambition behind such a strange and unconventional story, but I also can't pretend I enjoyed the reading experience as much as I wanted to. I spent too much time confused, trying to make sense of what I was reading instead of actually enjoying it. The premise pulled me in, but the execution left me feeling disconnected, and by the end, I was more relieved to be finished than blown away by what I had read. Sadly, this one just wasn't for me.

ADDENDUM
 
Given the uncertainty many readers are experiencing with this novel I thought some thoughts in the author’s own words would be useful.
 
I first came across M John Harrison’s work in his short story collection “You Should Come With Me Now” (2017) and one of those stories “The Crisis” was the forerunner of this novel. I have also read his so-called anti-memoir “Wish I Was Here” and its worth including some quotes from that book as it explains much of his writing philosophy.
 
In one early quote he says  
“An editor once told me … There's a perfectly good plot behind your novel, it's just that the author has taken most of it out: I thought that astonishingly perceptive until I realised that it wasn't supposed to be a compliment. That was before I moved on to the stage of not putting most of it in.”

 
Later he talks about
 
“Structures broken or fallen, structures bricolaged on to one another, …  which don't just undercut expectation but which seem to make nothing you can know. Instead they struggle to imply there's something different - some different way of describing things - to be had. This descriptive system could be accessed, you believe, if only you knew how to assemble the items and forms of the text as a key. The text clings to this belief too, despite bizarreness all around. There is just enough of a common motive to keep the relationship going, keep up the exploration of the territory.”

 
And
 
“Writer, reader and text struggle off into the distances they have constructed together, gesticulating and out of sorts with one another, yet bound by the idea that something can come of all this. “

 
The non-conventional nature of this year’s longlist seems to link to Patricia Lockwood’s comments “In the face of AI and the fractured attention span, I think we’re going to see a new avant-garde that insists on the participation of the human in our literature again. I feel there is a new, weird literature emerging that has a stronger human fingerprint on it”
 
M John Harrison uses “The Weird” to distinguish his later fiction from his earlier Sci-Fi
 
“A Weird text may not add up. It may not resolve. In fact it almost certainly won't. Nevertheless there will be no signposts, The author is not on this tour to guide you. The author's work has been to strip out affects, conclusions and motivations, then replace them out of order and at an odd angle. The way the picture is painted questions what's being painted; the things that are painted question each other; the internal lighting questions everything: any episteme you can assemble to 'understand' the Weird should fail; or even better, almost succeed:”

 
Note that reviews and media calling this a Science Fiction novel are wrong – a Science Fiction novel by M John Harrison (of which he wrote many in the earlier part of his career) would have a rationale and closure for the reader  
 
“Wish I Was Here” has a chapter actually entitled “The End of Everything” where he grapples with the form of “disaster” novel that the current malaise of the UK requires.
 
“As far as Cassandra is concerned. the disaster is that which has always already happened. What sort of disaster are we in? What sort of metaphors does it cause us to seek this week? … Who's doing for us what Nevil Shute did for the On the Beach generation? What does our disaster - not yet risen to consciousness but already, surely. fully known - feel like? Is it possible to describe a post-economic melancholy? What would be the landscape indices of that? What would be the indices of the collapse of the corporate management class, which has already begun to happen but which will be visible to it only in hindsight ….. Fictional disasters are animations of the deep social and political. anxieties of their time, and in the UK the age of post-industrial, post-Imperial melancholy, which was an anxiety of the modern. is giving way to something different, something which needs to be voiced before we can perceive it, let alone engage with it”

 
Finally my ARC of this novel came with a printed letter from the author which might be useful:
 
”Thanks for having a look at The End of Everything. I started this novel in 2010. It's a disaster story, told from the basis of a prolonged catastrophe, one that would, to its victims, seen to unfold so slowly, that they were like the proverbial frogs in the pot: not noticing that their world is changing.
 
This enabled me to mirror our current situation, and develop themes of environmental degradation and cultural change. There are Brexit-linked sub-themes; and a strand that presents alien invasion as gentrification.
 
The End of Everything is set in the seaside "art towns" of the South Eastern UK, all the way round from Hastings to Margate, where gentrification and generational change - a slow disaster for the culture of the original inhabitants - are still being worked through.
 
I wanted the book to be dark. Both a tragedy and a comedy. Landscape-oriented. And with strong elements of satire, parody and mischievousness. I wanted a limited number of characters and a simple picaresque structure. A journey along a coastline in the process of being geographically changed. A collision between the Gothic, the road movie and the seaside novel.
 
I hope you'll feel empathy with Marnie and her nephew Phillip and their confused attempts to understand a world which is no longer understandable, no longer theirs. I hope too that you'll feel a creepy delight at the behaviour of our alien overlords, whose origins and motives are impossible to understand.


ORIGINAL REVIEW

For the rest of the journey, it slept fitfully, waking suddenly for no reason, staring excitedly out of the window as if it had seen something it recognised, then losing energy and looking around with a helpless air. Tennent was tired too. In retrospect, the bad patch puzzled him. It had seemed so pointedly aimed at him, yet so personally irrelevant: though it presented as one, it wasn't a memory. Generally, a patch had a more public narrative as well as a more obvious one. It placed you in the position of an accidental spectator. Everyone saw roughly the same thing, as though the world was patiently replaying an extract from its repertoire for an audience that would never quite understand it.

 
MJ Harrison is a hugely versatile writer – winner of the 2007 Arthur C Clarke and Philip K Dick awards for his Science Fiction novel “Nova Swing” (second in a trilogy – the first in 2002, the last in 2012) and the 2020 Goldsmith Prize for his previous novel (his first since that trilogy) “The Sunken Land Beings To Rise Again” which I described as Sarah Perry, Esther Kinsky and Daisy Johnson thrown in a food blender with a dash or Dr Who and as a modern time-set parable on Brexit/Populism and the way in which London based middle classes did not follow the drivers of these trends (or their complicity in them).
 
And this novel I think is more in the spirit of that last book. 

Set here in a near future, post-apocalypse in many ways but a slow-burn apocalypse whose actual nature and ramifications are still rather mysterious to those who are living through it. 

And I think also serving as a part-satire, part-analogy on ideas like Brexit, gentrification (particularly of the seaside towns of the South East, and by the artist class as well as the rich), the role of the City in the UK economy, environmental degradation and possibly (although I think this is a stretch) on the increasing gap between the Global Tech Elite and the general population.
 
For me also it serves best of all as a parable on the seemingly inexorable downward trend of the British (I perhaps should say English as this feels a very white and English book – like his previous one the only non-white characters seem to be aliens) body politic and economy – a theme which seems more pertinent than ever as have yet another seeming failure of a prime minister – a sequence in which the main common factor seems to be the country itself.
 
From the extent we can understand the catastrophe it appears to involve some non-terrestrial beings the iGhetti (from another “astral plane” and initially manifesting as a form of slime/jelly in and near water) but whose actual purpose (or even interest in England) is unclear, but whose presence centred on the City has rendered the government ineffective as well as leading to a series of “bad patches” – seeming glitches in reality or possibly projections from another reality. 

Added note: As my brother pointed out to out below this background is taken from the author’s short story “The Crisis” from the collection of his writing - and the first time I encountered him - in “You Should Come With Me Now”.
 
Previous attempts around the coast to stop migration have been reversed – with people instead despairing of England and trying to get abroad, a task made rather tricky by the complete lack of news from overseas, in fact (in what for me was an amusing link to the famous if possibly apocryphal headline “Fog in Channel, Continent Cut Off”) the European continent appears to have been misplaced entirely. 
 
The novel has two main characters – Philipp Tennant who lives as a beachcomber on the tideline, looking out for the various oddities which wash up on the shore from the seas seemingly full of odd creatures – and at the novel’s opening he finds some form of living artefact which grows before his eyes  - and quits his role trying to find some way to dispose of it.   The second characters – with who he interacts and who later inherits both the creature and the narrative – is his Aunt Marnie – and sets out on something of a seaside road trip.
 
My favourite passage was this one:
 
Everyone knew someone who, unable to bear any longer the loss of their routine, had found their way back into the invasion site, to re-emerge weeks or even months later atter wandering puzzledly about the empty towers, lost souls eyeing other lost souls in the deserted corridors and partner washrooms. With a decent pair of binoculars, you could see them from Jack and Alice's garden, staring out of the Lloyd’s lifts - which still travelled in their stately way up and down the outside of the structure - in despair. In a way, the Lloyd’s building, designed to question the relationship between the inside and the outside, remained the great metaphor of the disaster. It was the zone's dead centre in that sense, even though it lay towards the western edge.

 
Overall this is a eerie but engrossing novel – stronger in my view than its predecessor.
 
My thanks to Serpents Tail for an ARC.

You say: We're suspended in this new catastrophe. We're held in suspension. It's ongoing and unresolved. It never ends. The nature of the catastrophe is that it has no nature. Not for us. It's permanent, but I don't mean that in Walter Benjamin's sense? Well, in what sense do you mean? The only other sense seems to be that everything has gone wrong and we all hate it.
 
We were all of us, at least in the beginning, Marnie thought, thrust back inside ourselves by circumstance. Back into the refuge of our humanity because we thought we could now be certain there was something outside it; something which, though it was here now fully extant and recognisable, might not entirely understand that we were here too. The contemporary fear was that we might have brought all this - whatever it was - on ourselves.

Review:

While reading this novel, I’ve remembered a poem written in the 60s by a Soviet female writer:

‘As someone rightly noted, for ages already, there has been this strong cave wind blowing our way, for ages there has been a burning smell in the air; but we are stubbornly passing it by as if it is not there, and keep rushing off to the beach, to work, to the movies.’


What initially surprised me is that the feeling so strongly conveyed by this novel was there already at least half a century ago in a very different setting and very different place. This feeling of existential uneasiness with environment but also human ability to deny and to adapt. Maybe, only maybe, this foreboding of catastrophe has been a part of a human condition through all the history. It is also certain that many catastrophes are taking place for some people on this planet this very moment: wars, genocides, fires. And for those people, those individual human beings, it is not the question of choice how to survive, it is the way of existence. But they still try to live 'normal life', even to make some plans, even if they know the future may never come.

Back to the book. Normally I do not follow book prizes and my personal choice of contemporary fiction tends to have a zero overlap with the Booker list. But i was surprised to discover that this time two books i’ve read earlier in the year have somehow made it to the list. Interestingly, those were the two out only four recent contemporary titles i’ve read. Encouraged by such a success rate, I’ve sampled some other titles on the list and this one has grabbed me by its first sentence:

'For years Phillip Tennent lived at the edge of the sea where artefacts were washed up every day.'

The sentence by itself has already told a mini-story. There was a promise and an intrigue in juxtaposing almost idyllic image with some kind of unknown source of uneasiness bordering on an alarm. So i’ve taken the plunge.

I can now report that what I’ve just read was sufficiently bonkers, but in a good way, very well written, poetic at places but a bit lacking on originality front as these type of novels go. (It is an open question though how a dystopian novel could be original in the current world). However, I’ve enjoyed reading it. I liked its deliberate incoherence. And I feel sufficiently intrigued to read the author’s earlier work sometime soon to understand his whole project better. I felt this work was an addition to some textual universe he had created before.

This novel has got a strong echo of J.G. Ballard catastrophe novels (ie High-Rise): familiar places are deliberately defamiliarised; dilapidated post-industrial landscapes; people who have just survived some kind of a catastrophe or might live under the shadow of a threat they do not totally understand. Apart from Ballard, Tarkovsky’s Stalker comes to mind as well if one set ‘the zone’ in a coastal area of North Kent (the UK).

The motive of pointless violence committed by the most vulnerable people is the strong one. Arguably such people are assumed to be the most innocent, who are usually more suitable for the role of the victims. So here we are for a shock factor. In this respect the book has reminded me of László Krasznahorkai, the recent Nobel laureate earlier described by Susan Sontag as the contemporary Hungarian master of the apocalypse. Nothing could compare with his character from Satantango, a seven year old child, torturing her pet cat and forcefully feeding it to death with poisoned milk. But a slightly demented granny with a kitchen knife punishing a teen to death and then going on a killing spree with a gun might not be too far. Characteristically, in both cases they end up killing the creature they love the most.

This is a kind of a novel that is temporarily set in a twilight zone of some apocalypse. This is the time of decadence before some possible new meaning would appear from the ruins of the old. More fortunate people would either be able to escape or successfully fence their surroundings. Less fortunate people might obtain a license to be savages for a time being (even and maybe more so if they are elderly artists).

The basic set up of this novel is more or less straightforward: a dystopian present; there is some phenomenon called ‘ighetty’ (imho, not very imaginatively). No one knows what it is but this thing seems to invade the city of London and they shoot at it just in case. Not that far away in the river's estuary and the coastal area, three main characters Philip, the beachcomber, his aunt and the artefact Philip has found in the sea try to hang on or maybe escape by moving around and getting themselves in different ‘situations’. The chapters are short, impressionistic and only loosely connected. They are more like scenes, snapshots, the matter; distinct points in this weird space and time. Some of them are letters, extracts from a diary or some sort of flashbacks.

This is for example a beautiful intermission of the one of the chapters:

Everything in the picture has been driven mad by the light. Do you see? The ocean, the beach, the people, the objects. The frozen yet energetic motion of the waves, clouds, bathers, ships, everything is an accident of the painted light - a pure 1930s light, from an imagination stocked when people could use words like ‘spiritual’ and still mean something.


This is the one of those novels that is very likely meant to be ‘co-written’ by the reader in the sense of Roland Barthes (his death of the author business). Every reader would enter into an imaginative collaboration by bringing her personal associations and experiences to uniquely interpret this text and to fill in its numerous lacunas. Respectively every reader is left a lot of freedom by the author to make a coherent story out of this if she wishes: to decide who/what ‘ighetty’ and artefacts might be; what those characters mean when they interact with each other and how all of this might end. The readers who like this kind of game and the subject matter would be pleased.

There are also a few “Easter eggs” scattered in the text here to be found. For example, Alexander Grothendieck got name-checked. He was the one of the creators of the modern geometry but got mad lately and has lead a secluded life writing some weird diary. There is a character in the novel who might be modelled on him. Another ‘eggs’ are Turing’s apple (poisoned), references to Picasso’s paintings and direct mention of Cy Twombly. There are probably many more.

The readers also can entertain themselves trying to guess where exactly on the Kent coast the story takes place or try to identify the latest social phenomena alluded in the novel: Covid; Brexit, small boats crisis and emergence of artificial intelligence is in there.

With the small boats, he did something quite poignant: ‘Small boats traffic on the Channel routes had subsided to a trickle then reversed like a kind of backed-up plumbing of the collective soul.’ In this world people want to live the island for the continent. Moreover, they are not sure anymore if the continent still exist but this does not stop them from going bringing up a certain metaphysical dimension to this in terms of their direction of travel. Again i’ve seen something similar has been done in the recent fiction already, but it is an impressive detail here anyway.

The word ‘artefact’ might be pointing at ‘artificial fact’. In this interpretation, an artificial intelligence is portrayed here, trying to appropriate human thoughts, shapes, form and habits, attempting to create a shallow physical likeness of a real person in order to replace him in the minds of the others, trying to please. But also some people seem to adapt to fall in love with it etc. This does not seem too dystopian nowadays.

In alluding to so many current social themes this novel has reminded me of Ali Smith's recent work. I first encountered M. John Harrison in 2017, rather late in his brilliant career, via his short-story and flash-fiction compilation You Should Come With Me Now, published by Comma Press and entered by them into the Republic of Consciousness Prize for fiction from small presses.

One of the more striking works in that collection, which Harrison also included in the curated collection Settling the World: Selected Stories 1970-2020 (also from Comma Press) was The Crisis (link to an online version at the Morning Star). At first read the banker-bashing put me off, but revisiting in the later collection, I saw the brilliance of what he'd done.

The Crisis told of the 'invasion' of the UK by the iGhetti, who ended up occupying the City of London, which itself turned into a form of ghost world (oddly prescient of the City during Covid), a form of surreal, disconcerting take on war of the worlds :

No one was certain whether the arrival of the iGhetti was an invasion or a natural catastrophe.

They resembled stalks of fleshy, weak rhubarb, which appeared and evolved very quickly from nothing, like the tentacles which seem to bulge out of nowhere when you burn a piece of mercuric sulphocyanate. You would see them for a fraction of a second just at the city skyline behind the buildings, just under the cloud base, evolving very fast like stop-frame film of something organic growing, then running out of energy, then growing again. They seemed like neither a thing nor a picture of a thing: they seemed to be extruded from a space that wasn’t quite in the world. The sirens would go off, all across the city from Borough to Camden. The artillery would fire and recoil, fire and recoil. The iGhetti would pulse and grow against the lighted clouds. Then they were gone again for another day.

Various simple beliefs surrounded the invasion. Some people associated the iGhetti with Dark Matter; some with the banking crisis of the late Noughties. Others believed that they “came out of the internet.” (Indeed, this was the favoured theory of the internet itself: the medium still firmly – if a little desperately – casting itself as the message.) While none of these theories could be described as true, they did, perhaps, mirror the type and scale of the anxieties that led the iGhetti to us.

I was drawn particularly to Harrison's views on genre, but one not delivered from the snobbish camp of a writer of self-proclaimed high-brow literary fiction, but rather from one writing within genres, the F/SF world and an author, at that point, relatively unrecognised by literary fiction awards.

Dividing literature into genres is limiting, a marketing device that got out of hand, and leaked into the audience ... A good ground rule for writing in any genre is: start with a form, then undermine its confidence in itself. Ask what it's afraid of, what it's trying to hide – then write that.

from the Guardian

My urge is less to transgress genre boundaries than insult them ... writing specifically for a genre isn't just reductive, it's an attempt to hide, a form of cowardice. It's special pleading, but it doesn't work.

That overdue recognition from the literary fiction world came with Harrison's brilliantly unsettling novel The Sunken Land Begins to Rise Again, a worthy winner of the 2020 Goldsmiths Prize, and a story that drew on one of the pieces in You Should Come With Me Now, Babies from Sand.

That's all a lengthy contextual preamble to my review of The End of Everything, Harrison's latest novel, and another brilliantly unsettling work which challenges genre conventions.

The author himself describes it as 'Conceived around 2010 as a book that might have been submitted for serialisation at New Worlds in 1967, The End of Everything is about trying to live as if nothing has gone wrong.'

When the novel opens, a decade or two after the events of The Crisis, Philip Tennant has been living on the edge of the sea as a beachcomber, biding his time, one of those 'waiting for the item that will give them pause for thought'. He finds it in the form of 'an artefact' - a humanoid/machine hybrid that grows visibly while in his care. He takes it with him to his aunt Marnie, a painter who has lived in the area for far longer, who decides to set it free while Philip is otherwise engaged. And the two then each abandon their homes embark on a road-trip along the coast - Philip in search of the artefact, and then when he finds it in search of someone who will buy it; Marnie in search of Philip, and then when she finds him in search of passage out of England and into the fog that surrounds in in search of the lost continent of Europe.

This is a beautifully written novel, deeply human, simultaneously compassionate and creepy, and one that speaks, subtly, to themes of Brexit, of gentrification, and of climate change. But one which also follows the manifesto of the weird that the author included in his anti-memoir Wish I Was Here:

"The space of the book wants to be big, empty, yet resonant, a space in which the characters don't even know they've lost their orientation. There's some kind of alien invasion or hauntological action or whatever going on in there, but even that's at one remove or maybe two. The space is loaded, but as the reader you don't know how to tap it for its resources; or complete it. You don't know what your side of the bargain is. It would be easier if you thought the characters knew the right kinds of things about themselves, but they so very clearly don't.
...
Weird text may not add up. It may not resolve. In fact it almost certainly won't. Nevertheless there will be no signposts. The author is not on this tour to guide you. The author's work has been to strip out affects, conclusions and motivations, then replace them out of order and at an odd angle. The way the picture is painted questions what's being painted; the things that are painted question each other; the internal lighting questions everything: any episteme you can assemble to 'understand' the Weird should fail; or even better, almost succeed."

In M. John Harrison’s 2017 story ‘The Crisis’, strange beings known as the iGhetti have appeared in London. Their arrival, like their very nature, is shrouded in mystery, yet transforms and fractures society, triggering an ongoing, possibly one-sided war. The city is decimated; misinformation thrives. Almost ten years later, Harrison’s latest novel The End of Everything picks up the thread. In some ways it’s an expanded version of ‘The Crisis’; in other ways a continuation, following characters in a version of England further entrenched in this new reality.

Phillip Tennent and his aunt Marnie live in a partly abandoned coastal town. Early in the book, Marnie responds to an intruder with shocking violence; this is how we learn this place (and presumably the rest of the country) is lawless. Phillip is a ‘beachcomber’, but the so-called artefacts he pulls out of the sea are in fact (we infer) living, growing beings similar to humans. It’s immediately clear this story isn’t interested in clearly spelling things out, in engaging in ‘worldbuilding’ – it’s strange and opaque. (A further example of this is that even when expanding the concept to novel length, Harrison never explains how the visitors came to be known by such a bizarre name as ‘iGhetti’.)

There’s often a sense in reviews of Harrison’s work that even those who adore his books – even professional critics – don’t fully understand them, and are just making their best guess at an interpretation. I’ve felt this myself. Though excellent, The Course of the Heart is one of the oddest and most indescribable books I have ever read; with The Sunken Land Begins to Rise Again, I was frustrated by the characters’ inexplicable behaviour and the way they talked past each other, as if each person was starring in a different scene. I have found this resistance to interpretation more effective in small doses, in short stories, like in the collection You Should Come With Me Now, in which ‘The Crisis’ originally appeared.

The End of Everything, however, is different. The strangeness and inscrutability are still present, but the novel as a whole is much more coherent. Harrison’s prose still feels to me like a kind of collage (I have no idea if this is right, but I get the impression some of the dialogue is stuff he’s overheard in real life and then worked into the narrative) while still giving you a sense that these people and ideas are cogs in the same big, perfectly constructed machine. It’s basically the fullest realisation (yet) of Harrison’s style and concerns as a writer.

Also, the landscape writing is gorgeous and startling and perfectly captures the world of the story, which added a great deal to the reading experience for me. There’s Marnie’s memories of mornings ‘like high-definition TV, wrapped in the documentary sublime’ (what a phrase!), cars ‘crowding around the village pond’ in an image that made me picture them as a herd of animals on the savannah; there’s ‘light as solid as optical plastic’ and a coastal view that appears like a hand in a sleeve, ‘the cliff at the end a hand clenched round something you might sense but never see’.

I will not try to offer an ‘explanation’ of what anything in The End of Everything is supposed to represent or mean. My recommendation is much simpler: I loved reading it. I was continually surprised and delighted. I feel if I read this again it might be a completely different book; I feel it’s a story that contains other stories within it.

I also must state for the record that, before I’d even read it, I was so sure this would be on the Booker Prize longlist that I actually looked into placing a bet on its nomination before the announcement (apparently you cannot do this). As soon as it was, in fact, longlisted I had the last bit of motivation I needed to start it. And I thought it was perfect: beautiful, funny, sad, absurd, strange. I would love it to win!

A worthy Booker winner, or the judges’ most wilfully perverse choice yet? Now that the 2026 prize has gone to “The End of Everything,” you’ll find readers making both cases — often loudly. At 81, after a long career adored in science-fiction circles, M. John Harrison has become the oldest winner in the prize’s history, for easily the strangest book on the shortlist.

And it is strange. Set on a near-future Kent coast, it imagines not a jackbooted government but a quieter catastrophe: society slowly surrendering its will to a force called the iGhetti — maybe alien, maybe born of the internet, never explained. I’ll be honest: reading it before it was even longlisted, I was bewildered and frustrated. And yet something in its vibe lodged in me — that uncomfortably familiar sense of sliding into a future nobody is steering while the rest of us scurry about in the background.

It won’t be for everyone, and I understand the hostility it’s met. But once you stop demanding easy answers and surrender to its surreal atmosphere, it captures our fragmented moment better than almost anything else I read this year.

This book is intentionally deeply disorienting. It reminds me of the old computer game Myst, where players are dropped into an unexplained world and have to try to figure out what is going on. Harrison provides hints about what has become of our world, but no direct explanations. There has been an invasion (of sorts) by otherworldly beings called the iGhetti. This event, called the Crisis, is already in the past. They have taken London, and the European continent has disappeared. In the seaside towns along the English coast, life goes on, but not “as usual.” Brutally violent acts occur, with few consequences. Properties of light throw up the illusion of buildings, or perhaps real buildings standing in another astral plane.

The storyline (if we can call it that) follows Phillip Tennant and his aunt Marnie. Phillip scrounges the shore and the sea for detritus from the Crisis, hoping to sell it. He finds “the artefact,” a leftover from the invasion, about the size of a human baby. It grows and changes throughout the story. It is a form of artificial intelligence fused with some type of lifeform (presumably of the same type as the iGhetti).

This book is not your typical science fiction, though it contains elements we associate with the genre. Harrison is deliberately disrupting the form. The world he builds is an uncanny version of our own, recognizable but altered by a cataclysm no one understands. One way to make sense of it is that the iGhetti have warped space-time itself. If so, the event is not just unexplained but unexplainable. It is outside any framework humans have for making sense of it, so they adapt the only way they can: by ignoring it. The book is about the human capacity to keep going in the midst of a new horrible reality, and its parallels to our own moment are striking.

I could probably write a longer review than the book (it’s only 200-ish pages) and still not come to terms with it. It almost demands a second reading (something I rarely do). I always appreciate books that make me think, which this book takes to the extreme. It deserves its place on the Booker Longlist.

4.5
