The Pirate Queen

Ariel Lawhon

4.31

Rate this book
Grace O'Malley was born to be at sea. Raised alongside her brother by their father, chief of the name of clan O'Malley, Grace learns early that her dreams of the open water are not compatible with the life she's expected to lead as the daughter of a noble family. And when her father marries her to the wrong man, a neighbouring chief named Donhal, Grace's world shifts for the worse.

Undaunted, Grace raises a family without abandoning her passion, aiding her hot-tempered husband in his campaigns against rival tribes. Eventually ousted from her husband's land in the wake of his death, Grace returns to her childhood home and begins her career as a pirate in earnest, claiming new territories and finding deeper love, only to see her livelihood threatened by English incursions into Ireland, and her family endangered in an attempt to check her power.

Tenderly observed and fiercely written, Ariel Lawhon's gripping latest novel channels the natural beauty of sixteenth-century Ireland alongside the brutality of the period as seen through the eyes of an unforgettable heroine and defender of her people, who truly earned the title 'the Pirate Queen of Ireland.'

A thrilling historical adventure inspired by the life of Grace O'Malley, an infamous Irish sea captain and folk heroine who risked everything to protect her people against the powerful Elizabethan regime

If Ariel Lawhon announced tomorrow that she had written an 800-page novel about a woman watching paint dry in the sixteenth century, I would probably clear my schedule and start reading immediately. She has become one of those rare authors I trust completely, and The Pirate Queen is exactly the reason why.

This book didn't simply entertain me—it completely swept me away.

Grace O'Malley is the kind of heroine who storms onto the page and refuses to leave your mind long after you've finished reading. Fierce, intelligent, rebellious, vulnerable, and endlessly determined, she is a woman born centuries ahead of her time. From childhood, she knows the sea is where she belongs, yet she is constantly reminded that her ambitions matter far less than the expectations placed upon her as a daughter, wife, and mother. Watching her challenge those limitations again and again became one of the most satisfying reading experiences I've had this year.

What I love most about Ariel Lawhon's writing is her ability to take a figure buried beneath layers of history and transform her into a living, breathing human being. Grace is not presented as an untouchable legend. She is a daughter struggling for approval, a woman navigating impossible choices, a mother fighting for her children, and a leader willing to risk everything for her people. Her victories felt triumphant. Her losses genuinely hurt. More than once, I found myself furious on her behalf as powerful men attempted to control her future, only to cheer when she found a way to outsmart them.

The structure of the novel worked beautifully for me. Moving through different stages of Grace's life allows the reader to understand how every triumph, heartbreak, betrayal, and sacrifice shaped the woman history would later remember as the Pirate Queen of Ireland. Each timeline adds another layer to her character, slowly revealing the full scope of her remarkable life.

And can we talk about the atmosphere for a moment?

The Irish coastline practically becomes a character of its own. I could hear the crashing waves, feel the cold sea spray, and picture the ships cutting through dangerous waters. The setting is so vivid and immersive that I often forgot I was sitting on my couch instead of standing on the deck of Grace's ship preparing for battle. Lawhon's research is extraordinary, but what makes the book special is how effortlessly she weaves those historical details into a compelling story without ever making the reader feel like they're sitting through a history lesson.

The political intrigue, clan rivalries, betrayals, shifting alliances, and constant threats from English authority create plenty of tension, but the emotional core of the story is what truly captured me. At its heart, this is a novel about resilience. It is about refusing to surrender your identity when the world insists you should. It is about protecting the people you love, fighting for your homeland, and finding the courage to chart your own course when everyone expects you to follow theirs.

The supporting characters are equally memorable. Some made me laugh, some broke my heart, and some inspired a deep desire to throw them overboard and let the Atlantic handle the rest. That's always a sign of a great book.

What makes Grace's story even more powerful is knowing that she was a real woman whose accomplishments have often been overshadowed by the men surrounding her. Ariel Lawhon once again accomplishes what she does best: resurrecting an extraordinary woman from the margins of history and ensuring readers will never forget her.

This is historical fiction at its absolute finest—epic, emotional, adventurous, inspiring, and impossible to put down. I laughed, I worried, I celebrated, I got angry, and I may have become slightly obsessed with researching Grace O'Malley after finishing the final chapter.

Without question, this is one of my favorite historical fiction novels of the year and another reminder that Ariel Lawhon remains one of the most talented storytellers working today.

A very special thanks to NetGalley and Doubleday Books for sharing this stunning period drama with me in exchange for my honest thoughts.

3.5 (rounding up to 4 star for Goodreads because I was hopeful and loved The Frozen River, and I'm giving Lawhon the benefit of the doubt that her next book will be better)

It pains me to admit that this book fell short. After many many many months of anticipation, perhaps my expectations were unreasonably high and after loving The Frozen River, I was hoping for a similar reader experience.

I’ve spoken about this in my Instagram stories, but my biggest issue with this book is the structure. There are 3 concurrent storylines: maiden, mother, and matriarch. It is frustrating to no end to get attached to a piece of the story, and then zoom forward 20 years, or fall back 20-40 years. It was exhausting which then made it impossible for me to get attached to the story, the characters, or the momentum of the climax of the story. Ultimately, all of it felt underwhelming. I think I expected more plot? More battles? With all the back and forth with the storylines, and the mannnnnny characters, it was really a test of reader strength to keep it all straight.

Now for the authors note suggesting reading the story through each concurrent piece, meaning, to read all maiden chapters together, all mother chapters, and then finally all matriarch chapters together. That means NOTHING to the reader knowing it after while reading the authors note. I do not appreciate that type of manipulation as a reader that maybe the fact that I didn’t get what I wanted out of the book was because in the authors note the author suggested to read it a certain way? No reader would know that when cracking the book open, and assuming the reader would is honestly ridiculous. If the author wanted it read that way, in a chronological order, then why didn’t she write it that way?

All in all, the story is rich here and it is always fun to image a Pirates of the Caribbean type of story with family drama, looting, battles, and the simple fact that women were sadly used as a currency. I found Grace O’Malley held at a distance where we really didn’t know her, even though the book was all about her. Her difficult demeanor was the main thread of story, and I wish we imagined her in more depth and detail.

I think readers will be swept up in Lawhon’s writing, but sadly, they might be left disappointed by the lack of cohesion in storyline and melodramatic climax.

To my friends across the Pond, Irish historical heroine Grace O’Malley (Gráinne Ní Mháille) may be as familiar as Amelia Earhart is to those of us in the States.

Having never heard of O'Malley, I was simply drawn to Ariel Lawhon's The Pirate Queen based on its title alone.

Lawhon's work of biographical historical fiction, set between 1535 and 1603, follows Ireland's iconic female warrior through most of her 73 years.

Daughter of the O’Malley clan chieftain, the novel traces Gráinne's life as a sea captain, clan leader, and negotiator under the pressure of Tudor England conquest.

The book's structure is episodic and non-linear across time, moving through the three life stages of a woman: Maiden, Mother, and Matriarch.

I have to admit, the slow, atmospheric pacing with time jumps across decades was a bit off-putting initially. But once I got into its rhythm, I actually enjoyed this structure.

Quite atmospheric — and at times brutal — Lawhon deftly weaves elements of Irish myth into the story of O’Malley’s efforts to preserve her autonomy, family, fleet of 25 ships, and Gaelic sovereignty against English subjugation.

Thankfully, the 500+ pages includes a cast of characters, pronunciation guide, maps, family tree, author’s note, and appendix.

If, like me, you're a fan of Wolf Hall by Hilary Mantel; The Pillars of the Earth by Ken Follett; or any of the Bernard Cornwell series, I think you'll find The Pirate Queen an enjoyable saga... aye, and rollicking good time on the high seas.

But as someone who reads a lot of fantasy, I absolutely loved sinking into this story, and this world. And the fact that Gráinne was an actual person who existed and lived this life, just made it all the more fascinating.

I also feel like for someone who has been looking for epic fantasy with a good romantic subplot, fantasy writers could learn a lot from the way the romance is handled in this book. What an absolute gem!

My only critiques are not really critiques but personal preferences. I wish that there had been a little bit more of the actual piracy explored. And there is a bit of head hopping and I just prefer to be firmly grounded in one POV at a time. But these were very minor things that honestly didn't bother me much because I was enjoying the emotional experience of being in this story so much.

Inspired by the extraordinary life of Gráinne (Grace) O'Malley, The Pirate Queen transports readers to the shifting political landscape of 16th century Ireland, where English rule is steadily eroding traditional Gaelic chieftainship and self-rule. But one remarkable woman refuses to accept the boundaries placed upon her.

As the daughter of a chieftain, Grace is expected to know her place. Instead, she carves out a life commanding both the sea and the respect of those around her, constantly walking the fine line between the expectations of womanhood and her determination to chart her own course.

Told through three intertwining timelines: maiden, mother, and matriarch - inspired by the symbolism of the Celtic Trinity Knot, the novel may take a little time to settle into. Yet this approach allows Lawhon to focus on the defining moments of each stage of Grace's life without becoming weighed down by the years that separate them.

Lawhon vividly recreates the sights, sounds, and brutal realities of this period in Irish history. She doesn't romanticise the violence, political unrest or hardships of everyday life, but instead immerses the reader in a world where survival often depends upon courage, resilience, and impossible choices.

More than a novel about piracy, 'The Pirate Queen' is the story of a proud Irish woman determined to define herself on her own terms. Although Grace O'Malley's life reverberated through both Irish and English history, her story has too often remained in the shadows. Bringing us on yet another historical venture is Ariel Lawhon in her newest release- The Pirate Queen. This time, she chronicles the life of 1500s Irish chieftain and sea captain Grace O'Malley (Gráinne Ní Mháille). Though little of Grace's story is found in Irish history, details of her life come from an unexpected place: her enemies, the English. Her memory survives, and we see her as a fearless leader by land and by sea, political tactician, rebel, pirate and matriarch.
Lawhon chooses to unfold the narrative in a nonchronological manner, rotating between three phases of Grace's life: Maiden, Mother/Maimed, and Matriarch. We see she was born with an unbreakable bond with the ocean, often defying her chieftain father.
