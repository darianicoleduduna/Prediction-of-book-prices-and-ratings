I thought I understood the Irish Potato Famine that killed more than a million people and sent millions more fleeing abroad. But a few years ago, during a tour of Trinity College Dublin, our guide paused to mention the Great Hunger.

He couldn’t have been more than 30 years old, but the fury in his voice had not cooled since the 1850s. The cruelty of the British government, its catastrophic inaction in the face of mass starvation — that wasn’t history to this young man. It felt as raw and immediate as the loss of his own siblings.

Maggie O’Farrell, who was born in Northern Ireland, opens her new novel, Land, in 1865, during the stunned aftermath of the Great Hunger, on the famine-scarred west coast of Ireland. Towns have shriveled. Villages have emptied. Rows of empty cottages have succumbed to rot.

It’s here on a damp, windswept promontory that we find a pair of Irishmen, Tomás and his dutiful 10-year-old son, Liam. They’re working for the British army, part of the enormous Ordnance Survey to map — and further subjugate — Ireland. Despite deeply conflicted feelings about his employer, Tomás is tireless and taciturn. Having survived the famine that took every member of his family, he knows the importance of steady work, the necessity of submission.

“He is the only one of their division,” O’Farrell writes, “who can measure and calculate, draw detailed draft maps in ink for the engravers to copy, and also converse with the people about where the boundaries lie, who owns which field, what this valley or that bluff is called and why, where might the ruins of this building be.”

I had been looking forward to Land (2026) by Maggie O'Farrell for months. Unfortunately, my reading experience can be summarized in one sentence: high hopes gave way to disappointment.

Land is a historical novel set in Ireland in 1865 at the beginning, in the wake of the Great Famine. At the outset, it follows Tomás, a surveyor and mapmaker working for the British Ordnance Survey, as he and his young son Liam map a remote coastal peninsula. From there, the novel gradually evolves into a family saga. I found the first half of the book distinctively better. I thoroughly enjoyed the evocative nature descriptions and the magical elements of Irish folklore woven into the realistic narrative. Magical realism resurfaces at the very end of the novel, though in a rather bizarre way.

The second half of the book feels as if it were written by a different author. Sometimes there is a palpable lack of direction in the plot, as if Maggie O'Farrell was a bit unsure where the story should go. To compensate for this, she introduces a relentless succession of misfortunes and family tragedies. This parade of hardships became insufferable and seemed to lead nowhere, making it feel as though the author cared mostly about giving the reader a repeated emotional jolt.

At a certain point, I noticed that this excess of misery began to numb rather than move me. I caught myself coldly calculating what kind of calamity we could expect next. I felt like a character in a video game, wandering around through a landscape of predetermined disasters and waiting for the next blow to land. Gradually, my sympathy for Tomás and his family gave way to frustration with the author. Don't let the lush, vibrant colours on the cover fool you—they bear little resemblance to the bleak world contained within. I was genuinely surprised that O'Farrell never provided a meaningful counterweight to all this suffering.

It was also vexing that the plausibility of several events felt problematic. Could a poor widow, who owned nothing but a single cow, really afford to take in and feed a family with three children for at least a few weeks? Would Tomás be able to rebuild the ruins of a house on his own, in such a short time? The narrator explicitly notes that the local fisherman's visits during the construction were rather social. Furthermore, the ruins were located on a mountain that required an exhausting trek to reach. Besides, how on earth was dead body identified when it was found several days' walking distance from home? The area seemed entirely uninhabited, and there was no radio, telegraph, newspapers, etc. Just a few examples. On top of that, there are coincidences whose probability strains belief—such as Enda and her grandfather ending up in the exact same restaurant in Canada.

Both Hamnet (which I loved) and The Marriage Portrait (which I quite liked) possessed a deliberate structure. You could tell there was a core vision that O'Farrell executed with ironclad consistency. Here, by contrast, I felt as though I were being led by a tour guide who began the journey with only the vaguest outline of the route and improvised the rest along the way. Ironically, the story of a cartographer and his family seemed to need a better map.

I truly envy the readers who were once again swept under the spell of Maggie O'Farrell's storytelling and fell in love with this book. I didn't, despite my best efforts, although there were aspects that I admired immensely. I adore Ireland and have long been fascinated by its history and culture, so it was a pleasure to read a novel set during a lesser-known period of the country's past. I cannot judge its historical accuracy, but at times, it felt as though the novel was set in the Middle Ages rather than the late 19th century.

Some passages were truly stunning, especially at the beginning, and I appreciated the strong environmental and feminist undercurrents. One of the novel's strengths is its title: it works on multiple levels. I particularly enjoyed the range of meanings it suggests, from the physical landscape to the notion of a Promised Land. The book is also highly cinematic; a film adaptation is likely just a matter of time. All in all, I'm probably the one to blame: had my expectations not been so elevated, Land would have resonated with me more.

A glimpse at Maggie O’Farrell’s creative process in her opening letter to her readers - where she was when first line of this story came to her, a story she knows she’s meant to write, inspired by her great-great-grandfather. From the first sentence to the last this extraordinarily beautiful and complex novel is why Maggie O’Farrell is one of my very favorite writers . It’s a profound, multilayered story telling of events in Ireland’s history depicted through the struggles of a family whose heartaches over the years become our heartaches as we share their fear, loss, grief, their love as they make their way in life.

In 1865 Ireland, a discovery is made as ten year old Liam is working with his father, a land surveyor for the red coats. They find a spring, magical waters perhaps that will forever change the lives of Liam, his father Tomas and their family in the years to come. The recent past is brought forward through Tomas’ memory in some horrific scenes of the Great Hunger, gut wrenching, heartbreaking and unbearable memories of the times of hunger and death when he was a young boy. Later through his wife’s unbearable memories we get a picture of the struggles and loss and suffering of her family. As the years pass, I came to love this whole family. I would be remiss if I didn’t mention the kind and wise widow who helps all of them through their struggles and their loyal dog Bran.

O’Farrell’s masterful storytelling weaves together multiple time lines and characters, bridging the folklore magic of ancient times to a time we recognize in the present setting of 1865. The changes in time are seamless. She draws on magical realism and this with the fairytale like stories of a millennia earlier never feel too much. These elements belong here . This is the story of the land, how this family toils to sustain themselves off the land, how Tomas has to reluctantly work for the red coats in order to pay the rent on the land on which they live by naming places and relegating ownership which he knows divides people, a story of the people who lived on this land before them, a story that comes full circle to the land in the future. I’m left in awe.

“A seanchaí is a traditional Gaelic storyteller or historian, serving as an oral repository.” (Wikipedia). O’Farrell is the modern seanchai telling the stories through the written word with beautiful writing . I predict that this will be my favorite book of the year.

Land by Maggie O'Farrell unfolds not as a single story but as a long, patient inquiry into what survives.

The novel follows surveyor-cartographer Tomás and his family after a mystical encounter at an ancient well on the windswept western peninsula of Ireland.

If you have read Hamnet or The Marriage Portrait, you'll recognize the way O’Farrell moves the lens, sitting inside one consciousness for a paragraph, then sliding gently across the room to another.

Here, however, she expands the technique across decades and continents.

Lyrical, slow‑burning, and emotionally brutal, it either lands for you or it doesn’t: some will call it a masterpiece and others will find its weight unbearable.

What stays, after the last page, is not exactly the story. It is the peninsula and the sense that some places are older than the people walking on them and will outlast the maps drawn of them.

The land does not offer closure; it offers continuation.

Apparently Maggie O’Farrell’s Land is being dubbed one of the best books of 2026. Well, I have news for you: it isn’t.

And I want to be clear — I didn’t hate it. I didn’t dread picking it up. It’s fine. Perfectly okay. But “fine” and “best of 2026” are two very different things, and the gap between this novel’s reputation and the actual experience of reading it kept nagging at me until I figured out why.

The material is genuinely interesting. It’s 1865, post-Famine Ireland. Tomás is a mapmaker working for the British Army — an Irishman helping the colonizers erase his own country’s history one anglicized place name at a time. His ten year old son Liam is his apprentice. Then Tomás drinks from a hidden spring in an unmapped copse and comes completely undone. He wears a crown of leaves. He denounces his colonial work. He declares he’ll create a secret true map of the land’s ancient history. A priest arrives, ties him to a table, and tortures him with starvation and prayer until he breaks. Liam finishes the army’s maps alone to secure payment and walks his shattered, silent father back to Dublin.

That’s a novel. Those are stakes. That father-son dynamic — Liam terrified, loyal, quietly furious, watching his father get destroyed by the Church while he finishes the very maps his father renounced — is exactly the kind of thing literary fiction exists to explore. When O’Farrell stays inside that tension the book deserves every bit of its praise.

But then she doesn’t trust it.

Take Tomás’s transformation at the spring. It’s the novel’s inciting event, its central mystery. Did the land itself reach up and change him? Is this madness? Is it vision? That ambiguity should be doing enormous work throughout the entire book. Except at a certain point Phina, his wife, asks him gently whether his old melancholy might be returning. His old melancholy. Meaning this isn’t the first time. Meaning the family has navigated this before. O’Farrell probably intended that as a quiet, tender moment of marital intimacy — a wife who knows her husband. What it actually does is accidentally deflate the novel’s entire mythic engine. The sacred spring, the exorcism, the visions, the obsession — and the man’s wife is essentially asking if he needs to lie down for a bit. Either the spring is real and transformative or it isn’t. You can’t have it both ways.

And that’s the novel’s fundamental problem in miniature. It wants two contradictory things at once.

Liam is the best example. Here is a character with a genuinely rich internal conflict — a son manipulated into the priesthood by a man who resents his father, slowly losing the faith he was groomed to have, thousands of miles from home in India when he learns Tomás is dead. That is a novel’s worth of material on its own. But O’Farrell gets bored with it. Rather than doing the hard work of dramatizing Liam’s psychology from the inside, she literally replaces him — his sister Enda steals his emigration papers, cuts her hair, dresses as a man, and sails to Canada in his place. Liam gets a few pages in India and an off-stage crisis of faith. Enda gets the adventure. It’s not a bold structural choice. It’s avoidance. O’Farrell took a compelling character with a genuine internal conflict and decided two people could share the role instead of one person having to carry it.

The novel keeps pulling away from what’s working to flash back to Tomás’s childhood in the workhouse, to an ancient girl named Brith sacrificed at the same spring millennia ago, to historical interludes about the Famine, the Vikings, the dissolution of the monasteries. Some of this pays off. The Brith material has real mythic weight. But O’Farrell signals every connection so heavily — the parallels between Brith and the family, the maps as colonialism, the land remembering — that the reader never gets to feel smart. You get told the answer before you’ve asked the question. Father Joseph grooms Liam for the priesthood as revenge against Tomás — and O’Farrell just tells you that, flat out, instead of letting you watch the manipulation and arrive there yourself. That’s the whole disease in one sentence.

Family sagas can work without a single protagonist. But only when the structure itself is the point. Here it reads less like a deliberate formal choice and more like O’Farrell following whoever interested her that week, handing off the burden of the story whenever it gets hard.

The novel has the shape of a major literary achievement — vast scope, mythic resonance, Big Themes about colonialism and land and faith. Critics respond to the shape. But the execution doesn’t match it. Too strange and sprawling to be a satisfying page-turner. Too anxious and explanatory to be genuine formal daring. A book that looks like it’s doing something difficult while actually doing something much safer.

Pick one, and commit.

You know, as I do, that was another day that changed everything.

I had this novel on my hold list at my local library and was surprised to get it so quickly (published June 2nd). Maggie O’Farrell sets up a tale about a mapmaker, Tomás, in 1865 on Ireland’s Dingle Peninsula in County Kerry. What I like most is how she builds the characters and relationships of Tomas’s family, his wife, and children. They are like separate pieces of a jigsaw puzzle that she fits together to create a dynamic landscape of interweaving personalities. We see in particular how Liam’s identity revolves around his struggles with his father. Tomás wants his son to follow in his steps as a mapmaker. Mapmaking was an identity that Tomás fully embodied. It enabled his rise from poverty, and he sees it as powerful. He also sees it as a way to resist British occupiers, the very men for whom he works for the Ordnance Survey project.

I am making my own map. I will never cede to their version of geography, of history, of linguistics and toponomy.

Isn’t this a characteristic that most of us have? The desire to walk our own path, not one that’s pocked with the false narratives of others. To draw our own maps is an act of affirmation. Sometimes we have to walk the path to know what it is, where it leads.

O’Farrell dips from the present of 1865 back into Tomás’s childhood, and carries us along lightly, birds riding on the feathery wings of her writing. Her transitions are so smooth, we fly from one point in time to another. Sometimes her prose is so beautiful, I float in an airy dream, not wanting to move on, but hover over a scene, a hummingbird sipping the bittersweet that locks me into the emotional life of Tomás as a young boy, his dreams, the nature of his creativity, his longings. Tomás doesn’t remember the years of the Great Hunger while he is living at the workhouse, but he is planning with all that is within him to escape. He is an adult when the haunting memories resurface and exact harrowing consequences.

A taciturn man, Tomás is haunted by the ghosts of his past, even before remembering who those ghosts were. His wife also came through the Great Hunger and lost a mother and many siblings, a father who disappeared. They are cut off from their past, from parental love and support, but together they love their children the best that they can. From these lonely parents, O’Farrell fashions a family. She sculpts them all with words. Enda is the firstborn, a year older than Liam. She wishes to be acknowledged as smart and capable as her brother, Liam. Then, there is Rose, a beauty who shadows all her mother’s steps and becomes the keeper of the hearth. Lastly, a baby boy, who seems to soak the land up into his being.

This is a story about how we are molded by families and circumstance, by the land we love and live upon. I thought about how my own experiences with the land shaped me; about how the homes we lived in, and the homes we left behind, had made their mark. My sister and I were much like Enda and Rose, in a time before the internet and too much TV, in love with trees and creeks, dogs, dolls, and playhouses in the woods, always pretending and loving every minute of it. This wonderful book took me to Ireland in 1865 and the years when Tomás’s family was growing up, and it took me back to my past, my own relationship with the land.

This house is a thing both ancient and disjointed, an entity of addition and subtraction, a palimpsest of stone and wood and caulk and mud. Its existence here, on the peninsula, is proof that everything was once something else: nothing goes away.

Land is an epic in the true sense of the word, encompassing not just a particular family, but a country, the world, the land itself. Magically written, with parts that are poetic in nature. It contrasts the impermanence of humans against the constancy of the land.
It begins with a map maker and his young son right after the Potato Famine, out charting the Irish countryside for the British. But soon it moves back two millennia to a time even before the Romans arrived in England.
At the heart of the story is a mysterious hidden pool that has been claimed by heathen and Catholic priests alike. The belief is that the water can give you what you need, not necessarily what you want.
Told through a variety of points of view, we follow the six members of the main family and their loyal dog through multiple events, mostly tragic. I have to admit, the dog was my favorite of the characters. Yet, I cared about each of them and was anxious to know how their lives would unfold.
There’s a strong edge of magical realism to this book. That doesn’t always work for me, but it didn’t overwhelm the story. It’s a sad story with little in the way of hope or joy. It’s not a tale of plucky individuals finding a way to improve their lot in life. It’s about the business of surviving day after day in often miserable circumstances.

Maggie O’Farrell has written a novel that is both a paean and a dirge to a land, a culture and a history. Her lush evocative prose elevates the land to a sentient being that emits emotions of joy, outrage and heartbreak. These elements create a narrative that extols resilience and the world’s natural majesty while excoriating the debilitating effects of colonial rule. This reading experience blends history and realism with a touch of folklore and mystery.

When land and history are involved, maps inevitably become a pivotal instrument that tie the two together. Maps have often been called the eyes of history and are the antennae that chronicle both change and continuity in world events.Maps reflect social values and shifts in power. The drawing and redrawing of borders on a map are a visual record of conquest and territorial control throughout time.

It is not surprising then that O’Farrell has chosen the family of a mapmaker to serve as the focal point that unfolds Ireland’s struggles under British rule.We meet Tomas in 1865. He is a surveyor and cartographer who has been hired by the British to map the terrain in the aftermath of the devastation wrought by the Potato Famine over the past twenty five years. Tomas, along with his ten year old son Liam, trods the land on the west coast of Ireland. The area has been ravaged by British neglect during the famine and towns,villages and cottages have disappeared, a silent elegy to the violence and injustice that has beset the region.Tomas is not well pleased to be employed by the British but realizes that his family must survive. Nevertheless, he silently bristles at the arrogance of the ruling class in excluding abandoned communities and renaming terrain to suit England’s purpose.

One day, Tomas barrels into a thicket in search of one of Liam’s boots. Inside the bushes, he discovers a small pool of water which transforms him from taciturn to wildly verbose. Tomas has become a changed man and informs Liam that everything is now different. He will henceforth craft maps that tell the true story of Ireland . It is not clear how this copse transformed Tomas. Might the copse been merely a spring of water? Or a “ faerie” well? Or a portal for time travel? What is clear is that Tomas’ encounter in the copse diverts the narrative path from strictly historical and sends it careening into past and future realms, introducing snippets of mystery and magic realism as an overlay to the main storyline.

In this way, the novel connects the world as we know it to Celtic legend and folklore. We become immersed in a panorama of Irish history, reaching back to the times when human sacrifice was a palliative to the gods and extending forward into the twentieth century. Embedded in this framework is the story of Irish diaspora augmented by prose that infuses history with the scents, sights and feeling of both oppression and fortitude.

The novel contains an epigraph defining the word seanchai as a custodian of tradition and reciter of ancient lore. This epigraph is a guidepost to remind us that ultimately, LAND is about feeling and emotion more so than facts and linear progression.For me, LAND is an example of oral storytelling.It is a reminder that we are a small part of the natural world and spend our lives attempting to unravel its mysteries.

Oh, Maggie O’Farrell! How do you manage to take a piece of history, breathe life into its forgotten souls, weave in a little magic, and leave us wondering how on earth we're supposed to return to our ordinary lives afterward? This woman doesn't simply tell stories; she opens a door to another century and invites us into a world where love, suffering, and the ghosts of the past refuse to remain buried.

Land is an ambitious, deeply moving historical novel that reminds us that the ground beneath our feet has witnessed more heartbreak, injustice, and resilience than any map could ever capture.

Ireland, 1865. The Great Famine has devastated families and scarred a nation. Tomás, a survivor of that terrible period, works alongside his young son, Liam, surveying the Irish landscape for the British authorities. Their job is to measure, name, and record the land, transforming a place steeped in memories, traditions, and generations of human experience into lines on a map. But how do you map a place without acknowledging the people who called it home? How do you measure a landscape while ignoring the lives destroyed by the forces that reshaped it?

THAT, my friends, is where this novel begins to work its magic.
Because the land remembers. Abandoned homes, vanished communities, and stories passed down through generations refuse to disappear simply because someone has decided they no longer matter. Through Tomás, Phina, Liam, Enda, Rose, and the enigmatic Eugene, O’Farrell explores the devastating consequences of displacement, colonial power, and the erasure of identity without losing sight of the individuals caught in history’s merciless machinery.

And what makes this story particularly fascinating is the way Irish folklore and the supernatural intertwine with everyday life. Mysterious encounters and unsettling details lend the narrative an almost otherworldly quality, reminding us that not everything can be measured, explained, or neatly contained within the boundaries of a map. Give me a storyteller who dares to leave a little room for mystery, and I’m already paying attention!
But don't expect a comfortable stroll through the Irish countryside. The suffering is real, the historical wounds run deep, and the injustices are infuriating. Yet amid the devastation, O’Farrell finds tenderness, unexpected kindness, and the stubborn determination to keep living. Sometimes survival itself is an act of defiance, and sometimes the smallest gestures of humanity shine brightest when everything else has fallen apart.

One of the novel's most compelling ideas is that a map can be accurate and still tell a lie. You can mark every road, measure every hill, and record every boundary while completely missing the heart of a place. You can erase a village from an official document, but what happens to the lives that unfolded there? The loves, the losses, the childhood memories, the people whose names history never bothered to preserve?

This is where Land truly lingers in the mind. It asks whose version of history survives, whose voices are silenced, and how much of what we accept as truth depends on who gets to tell the story. And isn't that just as relevant today? We may have more sophisticated ways of recording the world, but we haven't exactly mastered the art of seeing it through someone else's eyes!

O’Farrell's lyrical prose brings the landscape to life, while her exploration of family, memory, and belonging gives the novel its emotional depth. Her characters carry the consequences of the past in ways they cannot always understand, showing how one generation's suffering can shape the next. The narrative is expansive, and its measured pace requires patience; readers looking for a fast-moving historical thriller might occasionally wish for a shortcut. But this is a story that asks you to slow down, absorb its atmosphere, and appreciate the connections gradually taking shape.

Ultimately, Land is about more than a family surviving the aftermath of catastrophe. It is about belonging when your home has been changed beyond recognition, preserving an identity when others attempt to erase it, and discovering what remains when so much has been lost. It is about the stories we inherit and the invisible threads connecting us to those who came before us.

Maggie O’Farrell has created a richly imagined, emotionally resonant novel that confronts history's cruelty without surrendering to despair. There is sorrow here, certainly, but also beauty, wonder, and the enduring possibility of finding something meaningful in damaged ground.

If you love historical fiction that goes beyond dates and events to explore the human lives hidden beneath history's surface, put Land on your reading list. This is a novel that invites you to look beyond the lines on a map and recognize the lives, loves, losses, and legends no map could ever contain.
And when a book makes you look at the ground beneath your feet a little differently, you know you've experienced something special.

Maggie O’Farrell, you magnificent storyteller. The past isn't really past at all, is it? Sometimes it's right there beneath our feet, waiting for someone brave enough to listen.

Shortlisted for the 2026 An Post Irish Book Awards - Eason Novel of The Year
 
Maggie O’Farrell’s first seven novels saw her win the Costa Book Award (and twice be shortlisted) and pick up a Betty Trask and Somerset Maugham Award (both of which had more than one winner) but she of course came most to fame with her eighth novel “Hamnet” published at the end of March 2020, around a week after the UK entered lockdown and immediately struck a chord with readers around the world with its portrayal of Shakespeare’s wife Agnes and a plague struck Stratford.
 
Having immediately picked up a Women’s Prize shortlisting it was then a surprise omission for the Booker with the author’s husband William Sutcliffe adding to the controversy with a widely shared Tweet “I can’t really see what more a British writer could do to be in contention for the top literary prize in her own country” – a tweet both I think unwise and which retrospectively looks a little odd in a year the prize (admittedly dominated by American based international debur writers) was won by a similarly immersive narrative novel by a British author (Douglas Stuart’s “Shuggie Bain”). 
 
And it was not like the book suffered:  eventual winner of the delayed Women’s Prize (winning over two legends in Hilary Mantel and Bernadine Evaristo), winner of the National Book Critics Circle Award (the only major US prize open internationally), the Waterstones Book of the Year, the British Book Award “Nibbie” – and going on to net over 2 million sales and even an Oscar nomination for O’Farrell (and Oscar win for the actress playing Agnes) as well as a Golden Globe and BAFTA.
 
As an aside the controversy did lead to an unusual nomination for the Guardian Not The Booker Award – which otherwise normally attracted small press books – although the wise judges (of which I was one) decided it was too good for the prize.
 
Her next novel “The Marriage Plot” was Women’s Prize shortlisted (only beaten by the all conquering “Demon Copperhead”) and this is now her 10th novel.
 
Set in Ireland (for only the second of her books I think) starting around 1856 and drawing heavily on the history of both her native country and her family – it was explicitly inspired by her great-great-grandfather who worked for the British Ordnance Survey on a revision of the maps of Ireland in the wake of the Great Famine, with the author imagining how it must have been to have in effect been required to work for an occupying/colonial power to effectively record the terrible changes wrought on the human geography by the cataclysmic famine those same powers had allowed to occur. 
 
And it further draws (if I can be permitted a pun) on the waters If the sacred springs and wells which are scattered over Ireland – often co-opted by Celtic Catholicism but dating back to pagan days and water-spirit worship.
 
Her imagined protagonist is Thomas – a brilliant but taciturn mapmaker who we first meet through the eyes of his young son Liam: the two stumble across a small copse (hidden in a valley and previously not mapped – missed by his predecessors) and within it a pool of water – an encounter with which seems to induce in Thomas a manic verbosity (to the considerable disquiet of first Liam and then his family) and a determination to map his home country as it was before waves of occupation, going right back to the first inhabitants of the Island. A mania which leads to an exorcism and a life long dispute with a neighbouring Priest which resonates across the novel.
 
From there we follow – at a leisurely pace and with O’Farrell’s Hamnet style present tense and lush language and figurative descriptions (if it has to be said with a more explicit eye on a film adaption – the omniscient narrator can often seem like a film director and the script like screenwriters’s instructions) – the story of Thomas and his family, particularly when he decides to resettle them on the isolated West Coast peninsula where he found the copse and well (and where they are befriended by a widowwho forms a key character).
 
His wife Phina (including the back story of how the two – both effectively orphaned by the famine) meet in a workhouse, form a bond and are eventually reunited, setting up their own family but always aware of their past of terrible loss (Phina haunted by her memories, Thomas still searching for them but eventually literally haunted by his father).
 
Liam – who gets pulled towards the church and a Jesuit calling (some interviews abroad as he looks to leave that order form part of the novel’s scaffolding) – as a result of the deliberate attempt of the aforementioned Priest to prise him from paternal influence – to the despair of Thomas who had believed he would carry on his new project.
 
Enda – his rebellious and spirited daughter – something of a force of nature in their previous City dwelling, now resentful that Liam is the only one permitted an education.  Twice in the novel she disguses herself and changes places with Liam – the second time to take some emigration papers to Canada he rejects to join the Jesuits.  The twin swap in Hamnet was I think justified by its clear and clever Shakespearean resonance – here even the first time (and Thomas’s inability to spot it) felt almost lazy.
 
Rose – for a long time the youngest and clingiest but who as the novel progresses and her fellow family members either emigrate or perish is forced to take a more foregrounded role in both family and narrative.
 
Eugene – the youngest and only one born on the Peninsula, he is non verbal and fey, attuned to feelings of inanimate objects (for example he realises early on a wall resents its moss encrustation) but struggling more with conventional human concepts.  In many ways he represents the novel – which is almost entirely denuded of direct speech and similarly untethered to linear time and place, as well as perhaps the shared societal silence around the Famine.
 
Other key characters are
 
A buried girl Brith from an ancient (effectively first people) tribe (already at the time of her birth almost the last of that tribe, via her itinerant father); the girl and her ring first being seen in a bravura type historical sweep passage (reminiscent of the technique O’Farrell uses in Hamnet for both the passage of the plague and then the journey of a letter, but here with a historical rather than geographical sweep, anchored in place but very much not in time).  As an aside this is the third novel I have read this year to feature a girl from ancient times buried in a bog as a suspected result of a ritual sacrifice (here a fact) – after Katya Balan’s “Our Numbered Bones” and Sheila Armstrong’s “Red Mouth” (the latter of which has huge thematic overlaps with “Land” and is I think a better novel).  The ring, later discovered by Eugene, takes on talismanic properties (and ends the novel) – while the buried Brith occasionally enters the narrative to sense the changes on the land.
 
A giant dog (who does at times feature as a character and in a way more convincing than I might have expected and in keeping with the novel’s blurring of the boundaries between human, birds and beasts, inanimate objects and even the spiritual pre-Christian world – all of whom at times take the attention of the narrative voice). 
 
Girl and dog are historically linked “All that remains of Brith's people, and of her father's people, is a particular breed of dog, found only in these parts, with silver-grey fur and a heart that loves too well, and nobody knows why or where it came from.”
 
There is a lot to like in the novel – its rich and immersive, it some many heartbreaking moments which could be seen as melodramatic other than that they portrays the aftermath of some terrible actual historical events, it has a series of striking characters (if perhaps not always entirely convincingly portrayed – we are perhaps too often told what protagonists are seeing or thinking) which I think will linger with readers (particularly when we get the inevitable screen characters) and its overall ideas of the way maps are something of a Palimpsest of historical influences on a land is a very strong one.
 
Some of my criticisms are scattered in my review but perhaps my biggest relates to the book’s message.  I have no doubt at all at O’Farrell’s intentions and laudable inclinations – she is a passionate advocate for and protagonist of the empathetic power of fiction, and its reach across time as well as people’s.  In interviews for this novel (shortly after the Golder’s Green attacks) she told the BBC “I think all of us need to work hard at trying to teach our children and our children's children about not hating other people for being slightly different from yourself.".  However I am not sure she has really achieved that aim here – the British army (known only as “redcoats”), the Catholic church (both Priest and Jesuits), the local Protestant gentry are all presented as almost uniformly evil and with zero interiority or insight.  And in a related issue – the novel’s calling back to (her words in a NPR interview” the “very, very earliest people who lived in Ireland” seems to me again well-intentioned but also ill-advised given anti-immigrant events immediately post publication in her native Belfast.

Land's strength is in its thematic exploration. This book illustrates the memory of the land itself, how places have identities forged by their histories, and how the humans who live on the land cannot help but be affected by it. The story takes place in Ireland, following a brutal famine, during a time of religious superstition. Some combination of the wildness of the environment and the isolation of its radically reduced communities breeds a fervent and pious fear. The people are desperate for a means of power, some intervention in the face of the unknown, and religion rises.
