New York Times bestselling author T. Kingfisher returns to the charming fantasy world of Swordheart in this delightful and unexpected new romantic adventure.

Learned Edmund, a disillusioned scholar, is transporting yet another precious relic from one stuffy temple to a different stuffy temple. Another great adventure for the ages…

But when Edmund is set upon by bandits, in desperation, he breaks the cardinal rule and grabs for the relic, an ancient sword. To his surprise, instead of unsheathing a rusty, ancient weapon, an alarmingly handsome man appears, wielding daggers of blue steel and cutting the thieves down.

This man is the Dervish, an immortal warrior who’s been trapped for centuries in a sword of enchanted steel, and is angry as hell about it. He hates the curse that put him there, and he wants to hate Edmund, just as he has hated every wielder before him.

But the damned scholar is just so sweet and clever and kind. And while the Dervish may be able to protect Edmund from bandits, cultists, dragons, and strange inhuman diplomats, he may find it much harder to protect his own heart.

Okay. Let’s be honest.
When T. Kingfisher announces a return to the world of Swordheart, I don’t even read the synopsis properly. I just clear my schedule and prepare to fall in love.

And yes — it happened again.

This story follows Learned Edmund, who might be one of my favorite types of Kingfisher heroes: exhausted, intelligent, slightly awkward, and deeply, painfully decent. He’s transporting a sacred relic (from one suffocating temple to another — thrilling life, truly), when bandits attack and he does the one thing you’re absolutely not supposed to do.

He grabs the sword.

Instead of pulling out an old blade, he accidentally summons an immortal warrior who has been trapped inside enchanted steel for centuries.

The Dervish.

Angry. Lethal. Traumatized. Extremely done with humanity.

And understandably so.

What I loved immediately is that this isn’t some flashy, dramatic alpha-warrior-meets-helpless-scholar situation. Edmund is soft, yes — but not weak. He’s thoughtful. Curious. Ethical to a fault. The kind of man who worries about proper procedure while chaos is exploding around him. And the Dervish, who fully intends to resent his new wielder, finds himself deeply inconvenienced by the fact that Edmund is… kind.

Their dynamic is everything.

The slow burn here is slow in the best way. It’s awkward glances, reluctant conversations, small acts of protection, moments of vulnerability that sneak up on you. No forced destiny. No biological bond nonsense. Just two men gradually realizing they see each other. And choosing each other.

That choice matters.

The Dervish carries centuries of rage and damage. Edmund carries disappointment, disillusionment, and a quiet sadness about the direction of his life. Watching them navigate danger — bandits, strange political tension, creatures that absolutely should not exist — while also navigating their own emotional walls felt so organic.

And the humor. My God.

Kingfisher has this unmatched ability to slide something dry and absurd into the middle of tension and make you laugh out loud — then two pages later, you’re unexpectedly emotional. It’s not cozy exactly, but it has warmth. Even when things get messy, there’s heart beating underneath it.

Also — I love that her romances feel adult. These are grown people with complicated pasts and fully developed frontal lobes. They communicate. They hesitate for real reasons. They don’t implode over manufactured misunderstandings. The love story builds because it makes sense, not because the plot demands it.

By the end, I felt that very specific happiness I only get from her books — like I’ve been on an adventure, laughed more than I expected, and somehow had my heart handled gently at the same time.

This is romantasy the way I want it written: smart, character-driven, emotionally grounded, and just a little bit weird.

A very huge thank you to NetGalley and Tor Publishing Group | Bramble for sharing this addictive fantasy novel from my favorite auto-approved author, T. Kingfisher, with me in exchange for my honest feedback. I’ll happily return to this world anytime she lets us.

“Horror becomes valor if you just wait long enough. People turn sinners into saints”

I learned two things here. I really can enjoy cozy(ish) fantasy, and I am very late to reading T. Kingfisher. Suspicions confirmed. I suppose now would be the time to admit I haven’t read Swordheart…your honor, I plead guilty to reading out of order. In my defense, I’ve had Kingfisher on my TBR for a long time, and the premise of a fantasy with an immortal knight and a blushing scholar sucked me right in.

Learned Edmund, disillusioned with the Order he serves, is making one last delivery of relics before he calls it quits, when he is attacked by bandits. In an attempt to save the books, Edmund unsheathes a sword he is transporting, and is shocked to find that he is the new wielder of an immortal knight who resides within the sword.

I’m not sure I’ve read an author who is able to infuse a quirky story with social commentary, depth and humor in quite the same way. The major theme running through this story seems to be the fact that humans find many ways to use and “dehumanize” others deemed less than equal, and find any number of excuses for this cruelty: survival, gender, research, religion. Not so cozy. But it all comes down to choice, and Kingfisher actually left me feeling more hopeful than depressed.

It’s the characters that truly make this story. Edmund is awkward and filled with self doubt, but so earnestly good hearted that he managed to steal mine. The Dervish is jaded, charming, and vain, but also not immune to Edmund’s overwhelming goodness. Somewhere between their innuendo filled banter and simmering attraction I was hopelessly enchanted by these two. There’s also a touch of spice, which was surprising, but certainly not unwelcome.

Their accumulated group of found family also managed to work its way into my cozy averse heart. A sapient giant pill bug and a gnole, a talking (and also sapient) badger, helped to imbue every unlikely adventure with humor and humanity.

Somehow Kingfisher crafted cute characters and a strange, yet surprisingly realistic world into a story that actually managed to make me feel all those emotions that so many cozy stories usually don’t. I loved them. And I will definitely be going back for Sarkis and Halla’s story. Fingers crossed I also someday get to meet Angharad.

4⭐️’s audiobook. I loved Jesse Vilinsky’s narration in the Dark Inheritance Trilogy, and I was even more impressed with her range in this one.

Another adventure begins in T. Kingfisher’s delightfully strange fantasy world with Daggerbound, the second book in the Swordheart series. This time, familiar character Learned Edmund gets his chance to take center stage—and what follows is an adventure filled with dangerous creatures, unusual traveling companions, an extremely opinionated donkey, and a romance between two men who would apparently rather face mortal peril than talk openly about their feelings.

Edmund, the scholar readers may remember from Kingfisher’s Clocktaur War duology, is tasked with transporting a mysterious relic when he is attacked by bandits along the road. The relic happens to be a sword, and, with few options left, Edmund unsheathes it.

For those who have read Swordheart, you probably know what happens next.

An immortal warrior known as the Dervish appears, bound to the sword, and promptly saves Edmund with the help of two silver daggers. Unfortunately, the Dervish is considerably less enthusiastic about the arrangement. After centuries trapped within the sword, he is understandably angry to find himself bound to yet another wielder. At least Edmund, with his extensive knowledge of history, can recount everything the Dervish has missed—however awkwardly and at whatever wildly inappropriate moment the information happens to occur to him.

Between Edmund’s brain and the Dervish’s brawn, the two manage to escape one dangerous situation after another. That is, once they learn to work together with some semblance of trust. And with trust comes mutual admiration, followed eventually by something considerably more complicated.

The Dervish can’t help being charmed by Edmund’s nerdier tendencies, even when they become actively life-threatening. There is perhaps no better example of Edmund as a character than his decision to stop and sketch a dragon when getting away from said dragon would seem like the more pressing concern. Yet the Dervish also comes to recognize that Edmund’s seemingly endless supply of knowledge can save their lives, particularly when they encounter unfamiliar and dangerous flora and fauna.

Edmund, meanwhile, can’t stop noticing the Dervish’s physique or his obvious prowess as a warrior. Of the two, the Dervish is also far more socially adept—and far more willing to scheme his way through a problem when necessary.

As in Swordheart, the journey becomes even more entertaining as an increasingly bizarre collection of companions joins the travelers. Among them are Large Francis, a giant pill bug, and Dog Violet, a female gnole who quickly became one of my favorite additions to Kingfisher’s wonderfully peculiar world. Their travels also involve dangers ranging from murderous hounds and human sacrifice to a particularly horrifying allergic reaction.

Yet even when the characters are facing potentially fatal circumstances, Kingfisher never loses her signature sense of humor. The dialogue is masterfully funny, with even the characters’ muttered comments often worth their weight in gold, while their internal monologues are equally entertaining. This book will be particularly enjoyable for readers who, as the Dervish observes, would “pick maniacal humor every time.”

That humor also carries beautifully into the romance. Edmund and the Dervish have a great deal they should probably discuss and very little interest in actually discussing any of it. Watching their mutual attraction develop while both dance awkwardly around their feelings is hilarious, but what ultimately makes their relationship work is the tenderness underneath the comedy. They may struggle to say what they feel, but they become remarkably quick to show it. As the danger increases, so do the sacrifices they are willing to make for one another.

The audiobook adds another layer to an already colorful cast. Jesse Vilinsky rises to the considerable challenge of voicing this entire collection of humans, gnoles, warriors, scholars, and other unusual beings on her own. Through distinct accents, personalities, and vocal choices, she makes the characters immediately recognizable and brings Kingfisher’s dialogue to life. Her performance is particularly well suited to a story in which so much of the humor depends on character interactions and delivery.

Daggerbound combines fantasy adventure, romance, found family, strange creatures, genuine danger, and the kind of wonderfully absurd humor that makes T. Kingfisher’s world so much fun to revisit. Readers who loved the combination of romance, adventure, and eccentric characters in Swordheart should feel very much at home here. And anyone who appreciates intelligent characters making occasionally spectacularly unintelligent decisions in the name of curiosity or love will find plenty to enjoy.

That’s ex-misogynist, ex-pill, not-so-pissy Edmund — no longer Learned, even, because he has left the order of the Many-Armed God in despair over the impossibility of getting his fellow dedicates to join him in ex-misogyny. Edmund’s doing one last errand for his order, bringing some books and an old dagger to a scholar in Archenhold. This errand entails, technically, stealing a pack mule named Sweetness, but oh well. Edmund really likes her!

So, Edmund and Sweetness are set upon by bandits; in the ensuing mess Edmund is badly beaten and, while crawling around at the end of his strength trying to escape further blows, accidentally draws the dagger. Enter the Dervish.

The Dervish — this seems to be not just a sobriquet but his actual name — has been imprisoned in the dagger, just as Sarkis in Swordheart is imprisoned in the titular sword. And just as Swordheart’s Halla becomes Sarkis’s wielder when she draws his sword, so Edmund has, in drawing the dagger, become the Dervish’s wielder.

Unfortunately for their prospects of mutual understanding, the Dervish has had an absolutely crap time of it even apart from the awfulness of being trapped in a blade, in servitude to whoever wields it, and immortal. Just suppose that you were really, really into vivisection and you had a subject whom you could experiment on endlessly with only the minor inconvenience that whenever they died all you had to do was wait a couple of weeks for them to recover and then you could start all over again. The Dervish is a little bit bitter about his time on the receiving end of that enthusiasm.

Also, Edmund is not at all the Dervish’s type.

Eventually the Dervish is persuaded of Former Pill Formerly Learned Edmund’s basic decency and good faith. It also turns out that Edmund is slyly funny and that his disquisitions on topics of interest to him turn out to be of interest to the Dervish as well. As for Edmund, he finds the Dervish hot AF from the jump, which doesn’t stop him from appreciating the Dervish’s courage and sarcasm.

You already know where that all ends, but of course our heroes encounter many vicissitudes and make some friends, notably a gnole named Dog Violet and a gigantic extremely intelligent pillbug named … ah, no, I’ll leave that as a surprise. Ahoy there me mateys! This is one of the books in The World of the White Rat series. The author’s website says:

“The World of the White Rat is a series of fantasy romance novels set in the same universe, named after the Temple of the White Rat, an order of god-touched social workers, healers, and public defenders which appear in many of the books. They might also be called ‘paladin romances,’ since most of the books feature the angsty and the armored.”

I don’t tend to like fantasy romance but this is T. Kingfisher and I do love this world and its people. I agree that these books should be experienced in the author’s suggested reading order. This novel is book two of the Swordheart subseries but the fourth book in the overall series.

Learned Edmund appeared in the Clockwork Boys novels. When we catch up with him, he is exhausted, disillusioned, and longing for a new purpose in life. He has currently been tasked with delivering books and an ancient sword to scholars in another city. While on the road, he is attacked by bandits and the sword accidentally slides out of the scabbard. An immortal warrior, the Dervish, appears from the sword with daggers whirling and helps Edmund.

The Dervish has been trapped in the sword for centuries and Edmund is the new wielder with power over the Dervish's existence. The Dervish is traumatized by his past and angry at the control the wielders have over him. Frankly, he has no faith left in humanity. Edmund is absolutely horrified to own the sword and by extension, the Dervish. His common decency makes him a favorite character. As the story progresses, a slow burn romance forms naturally between the two.

It was wonderful to be back with Learned Edmund and his love of knowledge whether it is hearing about ants, local history, or trees. The difference of opinion between Edmund and the Dervish about Sweetness the cantankerous mule was both funny and sweet. I also enjoyed how the magic of the sword worked and the loopholes that the Dervish had figured out to his best advantage. A new character, Large Francis, was one of the best characters ever and I would love to have a whole story just about her and the gnoles. Old favorites like Zale, Sarkis, Halla, and Brindle play a part too.

The romance was fine but I have to admit that I preferred the Edmund sections far more than being in the Dervish's head. That said, I cannot wait for Angharad's book and adventures.

I did listen to this in audiobook form and thought Jesse Vilinsky did an extremely nice job. Her voice for Large Francis was just the best. Normally, I have no issues with Macmillan Audio's productions but this time there were some mixing problems. I am hard of hearing and several choices led to difficulty for me. When characters were doing internal monologues, the voice levels would drop and I couldn't always hear well no matter how much I adjusted the sound up and down. Additionally, a character is drugged and slurs their words. I couldn't understand a lot of that section either because the diction wasn't distinct. Hopefully, people without hearing concerns will be fine. Arrrr!
