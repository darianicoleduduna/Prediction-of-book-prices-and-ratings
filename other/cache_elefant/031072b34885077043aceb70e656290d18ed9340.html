Theo din Golden

Allen Levi

4.40

Rate this book
Într-o dimineață de primăvară, un străin sosește în micul oraș Golden din sudul Statelor Unite. Nimeni nu știe de unde a venit... sau de ce... Numele lui este Theo. Și pune mult mai multe întrebări decât oferă răspunsuri.

La cafeneaua din centru, Theo descoperă o expoziție cu nouăzeci și două de portrete în creion - portrete ale locuitorilor din Golden, realizate de un artist local. Începe să le cumpere, unul câte unul, și să le returneze „proprietarilor de drept“. De fiecare dată se spune o poveste, se naște o prietenie și o viață este schimbată.

Scris cu talent și empatie, cu o narațiune care aprofundează semnificația darului - oferit și primit - și a firelor invizibile de bunătate care ne leagă unii de alții, Theo din Golden este un roman despre puterea generozității creative și despre importanța mirării într-o viață trăită cu sens.

Published in 2023, I selected this book due to the average review for it being incredibly high, one of the closest to 5 stars I have seen. Upon finishing it, I wavered on making this a 2 or 3 star review (since can’t give 2.5) and finally dropped it down to 2 as I really just wanted to book to be over almost from the time I started it.

The premise seemed intriguing enough- an old man purchases portraits from a local artist, locates the person depicted, and bequeaths the portrait to them. The tagline for the cover was “Stories are told; friendships are born; and lives are changed.” Given the current atmosphere, I looked forward to a book that was going to focus on generosity and kindness.

But the first portrait that captures Theo’s attention is of a man whose child was injured in a car accident. We later find out it was caused by a driver who fell asleep and who was “an illegal”. The tone of this brief passage made me stop a second and wonder if this was going to be a maga supporter book. As such it made me feel a tad uncomfortable where this story of kindness was headed and exactly whose idea of kindness and Christian-like behavior we were going to witness.

Then the second portrait highlighted was that of a woman who Theo thought seemed sad. When he bequeaths her with the drawing, this woman tells Theo, a total stranger, her whole life history including that her cold, awful dad had “made” her get an abortion as a college student vs bringing disgrace on the family. She was now a successful CPA but was indeed very unhappy. She tells Theo she wants to be “just a housewife”. That would make her so happy. The way this conversation was written gave me pause again on so many levels. It just did not sit well with me at all. And it started to feel a bit preachy.

Soon Theo meets rascally Tony the bookshop owner. He seems like a big joker or clown always teasing others. He ridicules Theo for believing in heaven. It is at this point that I realize the author is indeed a religious man. And while the genre is listed as Literary Fiction, the book is definitely leaning heavily towards being a Christian book. So you know Tony is going to have a come to Jesus moment later in this book (pun intended lol).

Overall I found it tedious and just kept pushing on through the chapters, frequently skimming the flowery,extraneous sections of which there were many. The text felt forced and flat, and just, I don’t know, like the author thought the reader wasn’t that perceptive. I saw another reviewer used the term Pollyannaish to describe this book and that is spot on for me. The scenarios seemed pretty unlikely and many of the conversations were unrealistic IMO. I suppose the premise of the book is that Theo can elicit heartfelt responses from others but to witness so many strangers telling him their deepest darkest secrets and regrets and fears just was way over the top for me.

There were parts I did enjoy such as the empathy and kindnesses that Theo showered on those around him. For example he tells one woman that he picked her portrait as the first to be bequeathed because she looked like the kindest person of all the portraits on the wall, You can feel how that compliment touches her deeply. I also did like the ripple effects from Theo’s words and actions.

The ending felt abrupt but at least tied everything up fairly cleanly.

So while the premise of the story was one i desperately wanted to love and enjoy, the manner in which it was written failed to resonate with me. I don’t think I was the right demographic for it perhaps.

At the end of the day, the cardinal rule of storytelling is keep the reader engaged. There was no clear narrative that propelled the story forward in my opinion, just simply the promise that we might eventually figure of what Theo’s big secret is. For me, that wasn’t enough and certainly didn’t justify a 400 page novel. It felt bloated with brilliantly beautiful exposition, but not much more. All that said, the ending STILL had a wonderful emotional payoff, which goes to show how much more wonderful this novel could have been if it were edited down and injected with a bit more plot. I had some very mixed feelings about Theo of Golden, which honestly makes this one of the harder books for me to review. There is no denying that Levi Jacobs writes with sincerity. His prose is elegant, thoughtful, and often beautiful, and there were several moments that genuinely tugged at my heart. But as much as I admired what this novel was trying to accomplish, I never completely connected with it the way I'd hoped.

The premise immediately hooked me. An elderly stranger from Portugal arrives in a quiet Southern town carrying little more than impeccable manners, endless curiosity, and an air of mystery. Then he begins purchasing the pencil portraits hanging inside a local coffee shop, one by one, determined to return each to its rightful owner. Every portrait opens another door into someone's life, revealing memories, regrets, forgiveness, grief, love, and second chances. On paper, that's exactly the kind of character-driven novel I usually adore.

The problem, at least for me, was Theo himself.

His mysterious past is obviously designed to keep readers turning the pages, but ironically it had the opposite effect on me. The novel intentionally keeps him at arm's length for so long that I struggled to become emotionally invested in him. I kept waiting for the story to let me truly know the man behind the immaculate manners and philosophical conversations, yet every time I thought we were about to peel back another layer, we were introduced to another portrait, another life story, another lesson. Some of those stories were absolutely wonderful and deeply moving—I honestly think a few of them could have become beautiful novels all on their own. Others, however, didn't leave much of an impression and slowed the momentum considerably.

The pacing was probably my biggest obstacle. At nearly four hundred pages, I often felt like the narrative wandered in circles, revisiting similar themes again and again. Kindness. Grace. Generosity. Seeing people beyond first impressions. They are lovely messages—ones I wholeheartedly believe in—but after a while they began to feel repetitive rather than revelatory. I found myself wishing the book had trusted both its readers and its story a little more. Sometimes less truly is more.

That being said, I never found the philosophical or faith-based reflections offensive or preachy enough to pull me out of the story. Even when I didn't necessarily agree with every perspective, I simply accepted them as part of the characters' voices. Fiction should allow different viewpoints to exist, and I actually appreciated that aspect more than I expected.

Where the novel unquestionably succeeds is in its warmth. There is genuine compassion woven into these pages. It celebrates art, community, forgiveness, and the invisible ways people shape one another's lives. When the emotional payoff finally arrived, it absolutely landed. The ending was touching, beautifully executed, and elevated my overall experience considerably. I just wish the road leading there had been shorter and more focused.

Ironically, this book had been sitting on my TBR for quite a while until a bookstore employee practically scolded me for not reading it yet (yes... apparently my literary crimes are now judged in public!). Was it worth the public shaming? Personally... not quite. But that's the wonderful thing about books—reading is one of the most subjective experiences we can have. I completely understand why this novel has found so many passionate fans, even if I wasn't one of them.

Overall: Theo of Golden is a gentle, reflective novel with a beautiful heart and several unforgettable individual stories hidden inside its pages. I admired its message far more than I loved its execution. The elegant writing, emotional finale, and uplifting themes kept me engaged, but the repetitive structure, leisurely pacing, and emotional distance from Theo himself prevented me from falling completely under its spell. I'm giving it 3 stars—a thoughtful, worthwhile read that simply didn't resonate with me as deeply as I had hoped.

Now I'm even more excited to finally pick up The Correspondent, which has been patiently waiting on my shelf. Fingers crossed it ends up being the one that completely steals my heart.

But then readers started to notice it, and publishers soon began to notice it, too. When Simon & Schuster picked it up in the fall of 2025, it became a blockbuster hit.

Would it live up to my expectations?

Absolutely. And then some.

Eighty-six-year-old Theo is new to Golden, Georgia. He says he is there on business, though readers are left wondering what kind of business could possibly bring him there. Anonymous to those around him, he moves through the town with a gentle, meandering curiosity, savoring the people, places, and beauty around him.

“He gazed admiringly at the first touches of spring.”

But who really is Theo? And what is he searching for?

At its heart, this is a deeply moving love story — not the romantic rom-com type, but one that celebrates the unstoppable magic of human relationships. Lives intersect. Kindness matters. Small moments become transformative.

Through compassion, thoughtfulness, and heart, those people who connect with Theo find themselves deeply touched by the interaction. And, before we know it, as readers, we see change happening in ways both subtle and profound.

“Nothing is what it’s supposed to be if love is not at the core.”

Love is truly at the core of this story. And, woven throughout it all is the mystery of Theo. Why did Theo choose Golden? What truths is he carrying with him?

Be patient, dear readers. Eventually all the secrets will be revealed.

“Sadness can make us bitter or wise. We get to choose.”

Golden is an idyllic southern city in the United States but the community is fractured. Then Theo comes to town.

He's a quiet, unassuming man who firsts finds his footing in a coffeeshop, noting the beautiful hand-drawn portraits of community members. He makes a purchase.

“He was pulled like a marionette by the strings of music in his head.”

And then he decides to write to the individual in the portrait, to offer to give it to them, free of charge.

Some people accept, some decline, but all of them have a story...one that needs telling.

“A man who takes long walks by the river and reads books always has something to think about.”

On the surface, I can see what the hubbub is about this book. Each portrait provides us a window to peek into another's life, to see how they live, to view their struggles, and through interaction with Theo, to see their worlds be righted, just a little.

It's a feel-good book marred by tragedy and grief, and full of understanding and grace for fellow mankind.

And at the end of the book, we get a note from the author about how this was his first novel, that he wrote it just to see if he could, and that he was inspired by a real coffee shop decorated with 92 portraits.

So on the surface, it has a lot of elements that I love in the story...and yet...I just struggled to connect to it.

When I took the time to reflect, I think that really pulled me from the book was that I felt like I was being emotionally yanked for maximum effect.

Each story was selected in order to showcase a different struggle, vice, or victim - and every person benefited greatly from Theo's presence.

But the stories were also told in a way to really appeal to the moral high ground and the Christian mindset. And I really just don't gel books with heavy-handed pushiness.

There was the woman whose father pushed her into an abortion to "save" her career, yet now that she has the career, she just wants to be a stay at home mom.

A man whose daughter was injured and his wife was killed by an illegal immigrant, who needs to make a big decision about the upcoming case.

There's the survivor of the Vietnam war who no longer believes in God, but through gentle prodding and cathartic conversations finds himself opening towards the idea.

A homeless woman whose mental instability is calmed by Theo's presence and he encourages her to come to the church.

A fragmented community who becomes knit together by the benevolent presence of their mysterious savior who started the whole process by purchasing locally made portraits and giving them away for free.

If the author had just chosen one or two stories, then I could kind of get behind the exploration of the human condition, but given that there were so many stories...it felt like we got the cliff notes, the moral lesson, then off to the next one.

I would be interested in seeing what the author writes next but I don't like the way I was pushed in this story.

P.s. The golf ball scene. Just...I don't have words. I honestly almost put the book down permanently. That was a hard thing to stomach, and not an experience I'd ever care to repeat.
